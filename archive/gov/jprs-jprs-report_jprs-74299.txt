Sudan's education system has traditionally favoured general education 
rather than technical or vocational training. Sudanese labour experts 
estimate that the implementation of the six-year plan will require an 
annual additional supply of 5,000 skilled persons and, unless the government 
education policy is quickly geared to development requirements, planned 
targets will be seriously strained. Some 12,000 Sudanese are now receiving 
higher education in US, European, Egyptian, and other foreign universities 
and if the majority of them return home after completing their training, 
the supply of skilled and managerial staff will be greatly improved. 


Shortages in unskilled labour also appear; the minimum monthly wage is 
fixed at bSud 16.5, but this is not always kept. Because of this and the 
relatively higher living costs in urban areas, large numbers of unskilled 
workers often return to their villages to work on farms during the rainy 
seasons. Thus high employment turnover rates are common in both rural and 
urban regions. 


Much of Sudan's economic progress will depend on political stability in the 
Middle East region and inside Sudan. Wow Sudan finds itself in a conflict 
with neighbouring radical regimes in Ethiopia and Libya. Conservative Arab 
oil states in pursuit of their own objectives, are boosting their economic 
and political support to Sudan. Recent Arab concern over the security of 
the Red Sea and of the Horn of Africa, believed to be threatened by an 
increasing Soviet and Cuban involvement, has boosted Sudan's geopolitical 
importance in Arab strategy to reduce communist influence in the region. 
This has been reflected by the readiness of Saudi Arabia, Kuwait and the 
UAE, to foot Sudan's bill for arms purchases, which the US, France and the 
UK are willing to supply. 


Now the 1972 agreement settling a long dispute in the south appears to be
