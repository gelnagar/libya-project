LIBYA

TRAINEES BROUGHT HOME SINCE SWEDEN REFUSED MILITARY COURSES 
Tripoli Threatens Suit 
Stockholm SVENSKA DAGBLADET in Swedish 7 Jan 82 pp l, 7 
[Article by Margit Silberstein: '"'Telub May Be Judged by Islamic Law''] 


[Excerpt] The state enterprise Telub, in V4xjé, runs the risk of 
being brought before a Libyan court and sued for damages under 
Islamic law. That is the probable consequence of Telub's hav- 
ing broken the contract with Libya for training of 96 Libyan 
soldiers. 


Libya refused to agree to Telub's demand to drop the military section that was a 
part of the training and that was included in the contract. 


The negotiations, which had gone on for a year, collapsed a few weeks ago, and at 
very short notice the 96 Libyans got orders to return to Libya by 6 January. 


The negotiations have been characterized as "difficult and troublesome," and the 
Swedish negotiators have been met with a great lack of understanding on the 
Libyan side. 


"We expect that Telub will be brought before a court in Libya," says Seve 
Ungermark, information chief with Férenade Fabriksverken [United Manufacturing]. 


The negotiations between Telub and Libya have been going on since February 1981. 
It was then that Telub's new management informed the government that the training 
of the 96 Libyans included not only civilian but also military courses, the 
government immediately gave orders to Telub'’s management: Stop the military 
training! Telub responded by immediately starting negotiations with Libya with 
the aim of agreeing to drop the military training, which was primarily concerned 
with guided missile technology. 


Then after nearly a whole year the negotiations collapsed. Libya refused to agree 
to Telub's breaking the contract and dropping the training in guided missile tech- 
nology. At very short notice, only a few days, Libya decided to call home the 96, 
who flew from Sturup to Tripoli on Epiphany (6 January). 


The negotiations, which were conducted by Kent Sj66, executive vice president of 
Telub, and Olle Lund, director general of Férenade Fabriksverken (FFV), have been

characterized as "difficult and troublesome." 


Olle Lund admits that it is reasonable to believe that Libya was interested in 
precisely the military training, and that the civilian part was, so to speak, ac- 
cepted as part of the bargain. 


Seve Ungermark, information chief at FFV, tells SVENSKA DAGBLADET that the Swed- 
ish negotiators met with a lack of understanding among their Libyan counterparts, 
who feel "snubbed and thrown out of Sweden." 


"There is no reason to believe that Libya regards the affair as closed. We expect 
that there will be legal proceedings in Libya," says Seve Ungermark. 


Government Will Pay 


The government, according to Nils G. Asling, minister of industry, has assumed 
the responsibility for paying in the event of a successful damage claim. On the 
other hand, Telub will get no compensation for the loss of profit that results 
from the Libyans' having gone home 2 years earlier than planned. 


What the breach of contract means for Sweden's continued relations with Libya no- 
body can say. The Libyan people's office (corresponding to an embassy) threat- 
ened earlier that if Sweden broke the contract Libya would denounce the trade 
agreements that Swedish firms have with Libya. 


Industry Minister Nils G. Asling is hoping for a settlement "in a friendly spirit." 
But he admits that it is stated in the contract between Telub and Libya that if 
Telub breaks the agreement Libya will get compensation. The size of the compensa- 
tion will be decided under Islamic law in Libya. 


"There is nothing unprecedented about that. That is in all contracts with the 
Arab World," Nils G. Asling tells SVENSKA DAGBLADET. 


For a long time the so-called Telub affair was a two-man duel in the Riksdag be- 
tween Riksdag member Olle Wastberg (Liberal Party) and Staffan Burenstam Linder 
(Conservative),then Trade Minister. "The Libyans are soldiers who will get mili- 
tary training at Telub," Olle Wastberg insisted. Staffan Burenstam Linder just 
as obstinately asserted the contrary. 


In the fullness of time Burenstam Linder was censured for having misled the 
Riksdag and not told the whole truth about the Libyan soldiers. He was rehabili- 
tated to a certain extent by the former cabinet minister Carl Abjérnsson, who 
found in an investigation ordered by the government that Burenstam Linder was the 
one who perhaps did most to bring out the truth about the training. 


Karin Sdéder, who was foreign minister in Falldin's first three-party government, 
was the one that gave the first go-ahead for the deal between Telub and Libya. 

In spite of the fact that Benkt Dahlberg, then executive vice president of Telub, 
explained that it concerned training of soldiers with "a certain military connec- 
tion." 


Karin Sdéder refuses today, as she did earlier during the "Telub affair," go make 
any comment.

Tried to Smuggle Out Money 
Stockholm SVENSKA DAGBLADET in Swedish 7 Jan 82 p 7 


[Text] Malmé (TT)--Hundreds of thousands of kronor in 100- 
kronor notes, seven tons of baggage containing fireworks, large 
amounts of chocolate and tobacco, etc., and pockets full of 
100-kronor notes. 


That is how the 96 Libyan soldiers tried to leave Sturup, near 
Malm6, Wednesday [6 January] noon. 


When the Swedish customs examined the 7 tons of baggage that arrived at the air- 
port by truck, a box of fireworks was found. 


It is forbidden to carry fireworks on planes, and so customs had to check the en- 
tire 7 tons of baggage. 


When the Libyans were going through the airport check they had hundreds of thou- 
sands of kronor in Swedish currency--in 100-kronor notes. One had 29,000 kronor, 3 
another 15,000 kronor, and others 8,000 to 12,000 kronor. 


Only 8,000 kronor can be taken out of Sweden without the permission of the Bank 
of Sweden. : 


The Libyans' explanation was that they had not had time to exchange their pocket 
money when they suddenly had to leave Sweden.
