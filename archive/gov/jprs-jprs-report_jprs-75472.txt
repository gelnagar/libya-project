[Question] How do you explain your stand with respect to Arab relations 
and the incident with Libya? 


[Answer] We in the PDFLP are all for building Palestinian-Arab relations 
varying in degree according to the attitude of this regime or that toward 
our revolution, the national rights of our people, and our right to draft 
an independent Palestinian national resolution without any tutelage, 
annexation or pressure. 


Starting from this viewpoint, we dealt with the problems which arose 
between the Libyan leadership and our FATAH brothers, and we did not 
deviate at the expense of the basic position outlined by our policy and 
the revolution's relations with Libya. We feel that this disagreement 

is secondary and must not prevail for even a minute over the necessities 
of concentrating Palestinian-Libyan potentials in a front for persevering 
against the enemy, the Camp David agreements, the al-Sadat-Begin treaty, 
and administrative autonomy. 


Therefore we have tried to limit this disagreement, in order to mobilize 
all forces in the face of the enemy and to protect the perseverance forces.

Arab perseverance front. However, we must devote ourselves to the correct 
policy which will preserve the forces and not dissipate them in case of

JORDAN'S FOREIGN LABOR PROBLEMS DISCUSSED 
Amman AL-RA'Y in Arabic 26 Feb 80 p 5 
[Interview with Jordan's Labor Minister by Suhayr Salti al-Tal] 


[Text] The removal of restrictions on the immigration of large numbers of 
foreign and non-Jordanian Arab workers and on the emigration of Jordanian 
workers has resulted in an extremely competitive labor market, one that 
threatens the livelihood of Jordanian workers. In addition to job competi- 
tion, the influx of foreign workers is creating social, health and other 
economic changes. Although opinions vary regarding the exact number of 
foreign workers in the country, they are understood to represent 40 nation- 
alities. In view of this new reality, AL-RA'Y sought governmental views on 
how the authorities plan to deal with the situation. Our first interview 
on this topic was with Dr ‘Umar al-Nabulsi, the minister of labor. 


[Question] At a time when foreign workers are entering Jordan in unprecedented 
numbers, it is estimated that 350,000 Jordanian workers are working outside 
Jordan. Why are Jordanian workers leaving the country? 


[Answer] The real motive is higher wages. Jordan, for many reasons, simply 
cannot compete with Saudi, Gulf or Libyan wages. Furthermore, because 
Jordanian workers have special skills and an excellent reputation, they are 
actively sought after. They are offered all manner of inducements, including 
high wages. Our business establishments and companies cannot compete with 
oil rich countries. Because of this, and despite the availability of jobs 
here, our workers continue to leave. 


[Question] Are there other main causes for this? For example, is the labor 
law itself an underlying cause? Is there a connection between the exodus 
and the work conditions prevailing here, such as inadequate insurance, 
fringe benefits and other matters? 


[Answer] Primarily the main reason is the high inducements offered by the 
oil-producing countries. Undoubtedly there are other causes, The cost of 
living in Jordan is one. If Jordanian workers are given adequate wages, 

they will not leave. I do not believe the items you mentioned are directly

by the POLISARIO. 


Among those interviewed by Stepanek was Mihammad Lamine, premier of the 

Arab Democratic Republic of Sahara (proclaimed by the POLISARIO in 1976) 
and member of the Council of the POLISARIO's Revolutionary Command, whom 
the author met on the last day of his stay in the Sahara (place and date 
of the meeting are not specified). 


Asked why only two Arab countries, Algeria and the People's Democratic 
Republic of Yemen, have recognized the Republic of Sahara "althou 
precisely those states should have the closest relations with it,’ 
Lamine replies: 


"First of all, it must be said that most Arab regimes are reactionary. 
Many of their arguments are identical with the arguments of the United 
States. However, there are also nationalist regimes--such as those of 
Libya and Syria--with which we have good relations. We have also 
established contacts with other Arab countries. One thing seems 
strange to us. At the time we were fighting the Spanish colonizers, 
the Arab countries were telling us: Spain is our friend, that is why 
we cannot help you. But now we are fightiug against a regime that is 
an ally of Israel, al-Sadat and the United States. We cannot under- 
stand it.” 


Replying to a question about the foreign political orientation of the 
Republic of Sahara “after the actual establishment of the state," 
Lamine answers: 


"After obtaining our independence we will be, of course, a nationalist 
regime. Everything will be dictated by concrete needs."
