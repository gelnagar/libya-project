LIBYA

Al-Qadhdhafi Blames U.S., Britain for 8 May Events 
PM100953 Paris LE MONDE in French 10 May 84 pp 1, 6 


{Eric Rouleau dispatch: “The Countries Who Arm the Killers 
Will Pay Dearly for Their Crime”’] 


[Text] Tripoli — At the wheel of his car, a machine gun on the 
front seat, Colonel al-Qadhdhafi parked at the gate of the 
horse-riding center around 12 km from Tripoli. He got out 
beaming, waved happily to aides awaiting him, and strode toward 
the race track. A crowd of riders — officers from the regular 
Army — hailed him, using his first name (Mu‘ammar), and ran 
to embrace him and to congratulate him on “the victory won 
against the people’s enemies.” “Down with fascism!’ young 
soldiers chanted, while the Libyan “revolution guide” mounted 
his horse and, waving his fist, launched into a wild horse race — 
one of his favorite sports. 


It was 1830 on Tuesday, 8 May. Col al-Qadhdhafi’s personal 
physician, who was standing beside us on the edge of the track, 
burst out laughing when he heard the newscast of a foreign radio 
station on a transistor radio. The newscaster, speaking in a 
solemn tone, announced — in the conditional [tense implying 
reported speech in French] — that the Libyan Army had rebelled 
and that street fighting was continuing in Tripoli. 


The report was not entirely unfounded. That morning, at around 
0930, the capital’s population was thrown into commotion by the 
growing noise of artillery fire (probably tank guns and mortars) 
and bursts of machine gun fire. Armed militiamen belonging to 
the revolutionary guard, in uniform or civilian dress, spread out 
through the streets to stop traffic — causing huge jams — and 
to seal off strategic districts of the capital, occupy the official 
buildings, and politely ask pedestrians to return home. 


Initially paralyzed, Tripoli became a ghost town in the early 
afternoon. The streets emptied, the stores lowered their metal 
shutters, and the population hid behind lowered blinds. Then 
there was a long, interminable silence which gave rise to all kinds 
of speculation.

began to move up and down the main roads amid a chorus of 
horns punctuated by choruses of chanted slogans. Young people
