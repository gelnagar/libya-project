GERMAN BANK--(Richardus) is the name of the first West German bank to have a 
large number of its shares owned by an Arab financial institution. The bank is 
in Frankfurt and is a subsidiary of the investment group of the same name. The 
Arab party which owns [the shares], the Arab Banking Organization, based in Bah- 
rain, with ownership in Kuwait, Libya, and the UAE, announced that it bought a 
large number of shares in the German investment group, but neither the number of 
shares nor the total value of this transaction was specified. The purchase of 
these shares is viewed as the third step in a plan to establish the Arab Banking 
Organization in world markets, after New York and London. [Text] [Paris AL- 
MUSTAQBAL in Arabic No 258, 30 Jan 82 p 42] 


JORDANIAN ADVISORS IN BAHRAIN--Diplomatic observers in Bahrain have confirmed 
that King Husayn's recent visit to the Gulf state resulted in an increase in the 
number of Jordanian advisors working in the Bahraini armed forces, the internal 
security forces, and intelligence. These sources denied that Jordanian armed 
forces have arrived on the island to help the Bahraini forces in suppressing any 
sabotage movement which might arise there. [Text] [Paris AL-NAHAR AL-'ARABI WA 
AL-DUWALI in Arabic No 251, 22-28 Feb 82 p 19]

SYRIA VIOLENCE SEEN POSING DILEMMA FOR TEHRAN 
London IRAN PRESS SERVICE in English No 60, 25 Feb 82 pp 6-8 


[Text] Bloody fighting between supporters of Syrian president Hafez Assad 
and armed militia of the Muslim Brotherhood in Hama has presented the ruling 
mullahs in Iran with a dilemma. On the one hand Assad's Syria is Khomeyni's 
best foreign supporter, even better than the Libya run by the volatile and 
unreliable Muammar Qadhdhafi. On the other hand, Assad is fighting an 
organisation whose Islamic credentials are impeccable. 


Since Khomeyni and his regime claim to represent Islamic ideals and to stand 
for an Islamic form of government, they can hardly express themselves as 
against the Muslim Brotherhood in the current battle between them and the 
Assad regime. Their difficulty becomes more acute in the light of the fact 
that the Syrian president officially claims to champion a secular form of 
government under the banner of the Baath party--albeit its Syrian branch as 
opposed to the Iraqi branch of Saddam Hussain. In fact, the Brotherhood is 
using the same languge against Assad as Khomeyni used against the late Shah. 


Moreover, many of Khomeyni's aides, including former prime minister Mahdavi- 
Kani and notorious Islaxic judges like Sadegh Khalkhali, Mohammadi Gailani and 
Assadollah Lajevardi, are members of the Iranian version of the Muslim Brother- 
hood, known as the Fedayeen-e-Islam. Its founder, Navab Safavi (executed in 
1955) had close ties with the Muslim Brotherhood in Egypt and Syria. 

Khomeyni's mentor, the late Ayatollah Kashani of the oil nationalisation era, 
was an admirer of Hassan al-Bana, founder of the Muslim Brotherhood, as well 

as Mufti al-Hussaini, the anti-British and pro-Nazi grand mufti of Palestine 

in the 1940s. 


Despite such historic ties and Khomeyni's own self-proclaimed role of being 
the leader of all exponents of true Islamic movements, the mu

The “international" character of Iran's Islamic revolution and the contradic- 
tions inherent in Khomeyni's version of theocracy have been exposed by events 
at Hama. Many politicians, including some National Front leaders, did not 
believe Khomeyni was serious when he originally claimed he wanted to lead 

a movement aimed at "liberating" the entire Muslim population of the world. 
They thought that Khomeyni was wise enough to realise that being a Shia he 
was regarded as an unorthodox Muslim by three-quarters of the Muslims in the 


world. At best, they thought, Khomeyni could speak for the Iranian Shias, 
although even then he had strong competitors in Shia theologians senior to 
him. But Khomeyni was, in fact, dreaming of ruling over an Islamic empire 
expanding from Morocco to Indonesia and he was determined not to let his 
being a Shia stand in his way.

Some analysts maintained at the time that it was not merely a lust for power 
which prompted Khomeyni and his henchmen to enlarge the scope of their revo- 
lution but also the facts of political life. Khomeyni had combined his 
Islamic platform with an anti-imperialistic and anti-Zionist stand and at the 
same time he needed to export his revolution in order to keep the kettle 
boiling and divert attention from the inevitable problems that would result 
from the clergy's efforts to run a modern state. Moreover, he had a politi- 
cal debt to repay to Qadhdhafi, Assad and the Palestinians who helped him 

in his campaign against the Shah. 


Khomeyni and his close aides are known to be ready to compromise on some 
strictly Shia principles in the name of Islamic unity and in the interests of 
exporting the revolution. Last month Ayatollah Montazeri, who is a faithful 
Khomeyni mouthpiece, launched his Unity Week under the banner of "Sunni- 
Shia Unity." Since then the regime's turbanned representatives have been 
chanting the same slogan over and over again. They never refer to the fact 
that Assad is an Alawite belonging to a sect that is regarded as heretical 
not only by Sunnis but also the Shias--though Assad is said to belong to the 
faction that is closest in beliefs to those of Iran's Shias. 


Yet because his seat of power is in Iran and because he is a Shia mullah, 
Khomeyni cannot afford to go too far in his courtship of the Sunnis. Already 
he has made too many strict Shias angry by his unorthodox views on theocracy 
and his cynical use of religion to consolidate his political power--often 

at their expense. Nor are the Sunnis, especially the Muslim Brotherhood, 
likely to accept Khomeyni's leadership except for opportunistic reasons. 
Khomeyni is bound to encounter competition from non-Shia Muslim extremists in 
his bid to lead all the Muslims of the world.
