[Excerpts] A "report" broadcast Thursday evening on the radio newsreel and later on

Who planted the repr~t in the state media, and what was his objective? There are three 
possibilities: 


The first possibility is that the report was planted by one of Begin's associates so as 
to precede the cure to the ailment: If the Americans desire to remove Begin, then anyone 
who opposes his leadership is as good as an American agent. 


This method is not very original. It is reminiscent of the "Zinoviev letter," as well as 
of intrigues in which agents of one country strike at the embassy of a second country on 
the territory of a third country in order to create a conflict between the second and the 
third. In the long run, however, these stratagems prove to be ineffective and, if indeed 
one of Begin's associates planted the report, he did him a disservice. 


A second possibility is that the culprit is not one of Begin's associates, but the crony 
of another cabinet member who wishes to enhance his prospects. That is to say: The 
policy is okay, there is no need to replace the cabinet, only one man has to be removed 
and everything will be ail right, a crisis with the United States will be avoided and 

so on.

BEGIN TO PUSH TALKS WITH CAIRO APTER J.S. VISIT 
TA261345V Jerusalem POST in English 26 Mar 78 p 1 TA 
[By David Landau] 


[Text] The government intends to embark on an urgent new effort to resume the direct 
negotiations with Egypt. Informed sources close to Premier Menahem Begin predicted 
that this would be the practical response of the cabinet to the lack of progress 
achieved at the Begin-Carter talks in Washington last week, 


Begin is due to respond in 2 day or two to President as-Sadat of Egypt's last letter to 
him--as the premier himself noted in his airport arrival statement on Friday. The 
informed sources said Begin would try in his letter and through other means to get the 
direct talks with Egypt started again. 


The sources indicated that Begin would now be much more flexible regarding the format 
and venue of such talks. He would no longer insist that if the military negotiations 
resume in Cairo again, there must be parallel political talks in Jerusalem, He would 
probably agree to any talks, anywhere, to demonstrate that the peace process is not 
dead. 


These sources drew encouragement from Egypt's reaction yesterday to the Begin-Carter 
rift. They pointed out that Egypt had pointedly asserted that the peace process would 
still go on. 


V. 27 Mar 78 N 3 ISRAEL 


Egypt's current moderation has been interpreted by many observers as expressing its 
gratification over the Begin-Carter dispute. By proclaiming that it is ready to continue 
the peacemaking, Egypt is, in the eyes of many observers, seeking to induce further 
American pressure on Israel. 


The informed sources close to Begin, howeve:?, while not discounting this view, stress 
that Egypt could, had it wished, have conveniently broken off all contact at this nadir 
of Israeli fortunes in the U.S. But Cairo's relatively moderate reaction, these sources 
say, both to Israel's incursion into south Lebanon, and to Begin's U.S. visit, point 
unmistakably tu its desire to continue the peace process. 


In fact, these sources say, AsS-Sadat could perhaps argue that through his peace initiative 
the U.S. has been brought to greater "neutrality" in the Mideast conflict--and it is now 
for each individual Arab confrontation state to cash in on that situation and pursue its 
own negotiations with Israel. As-Sadat himself, accordingly, might perhaps see the moment 
ripe for proceeding with his own negotiations with Israel on the Sinai, reaching if not 
full peace then at least an agreement that could be billed as the first stage of a com- 
prehensive peace agreement. 


While the sources posing this theory concede that it's "optimistic," it seems that the 
government will now want to actively test it--if only because Begin is keenly aware that 
Israel must take the initiative in order to improve its political position. 


The sources close to the premier assert confidently that there is no real threat from 
within to his tenure or authority. They tend to shrug off the unrest in the DMC as 
pertaining only to the dovish fringe of that party. And they see Defence Minister 


Weizman's call for a "national peace government" as a "public relations ploy that back- 
fired" rather than as a major challenge to the premier. 


They indicate, though, that Begin was far from happy with Weizman's move.

ABBA EBAN INTERVIEWED ON ISRAELI-U.S. RELATIONS 
TA24UL830Y Jerusalem Domestic Service in Hebrew 1505 GMT 24 Mar 
{From the Weekly Newsreel ] 


[Text] MK Abba Eban, member of the Knesset Foreign Affairs and Security Committee and former 
foreign minister, is here in our Tel Aviv studio. We asked him to evaluate the status of 
Tsraeli-U.S. relations at this moment. Our political correspondent Shalom Qital speaks 

with him. 


[Question] Mr Eban, we hear from both the United Statec and the Israelis who have returned 
from talks there today that, in spite of these having been hard talks in Washington, there 
is no crisis and the negotiations will continue. What, in your view, will the United States 
do now? In what form will the negotiations! impetus continue? 


V. 27 Mer 78 N 19 


[Answer] I will permit myself to preface my remarks with a comment, The duty of the 
opposition, to which I belong, is the duty to make practical criticism. I say this as 
against the blind words spoken by Minister Shostak on the opposition and its duty to 
criticize, It is true that we are not taking full advantage of the duty to criticize 
with that amount of verbal violence which had been the custom for 29 years, but to 
propose alternative lines of policy is our duty, our responsibility; we are honored by 
it. As for the American problem, the word crisis is possibly not essential to describe 
the gravity of the situation, but there is a hard dispute. I have criticism for moves 
made by the cabinet of Israel, but I have no common front with the U.S, administration. 
On the contrary, the U.S, administration has, in my view, shown less wisdom and level- 
headedness than what could have been expected, and through this the U.S. administration 
contributed its part toward the worsening which has taken place over recent weeks. 
Therefore, I think that it is possible to renew the negotiations only if all three of 
the partners, and not only Israel--the United States and Egypt as well--if each of these 
partners takes some lessons in modesty and soul searching. The main task is to reach 
an agreed text of common principles, with the main obstacle, to my regret, being the 
inability to come to an agreement on the principle of territorial compromise in Judaea, 
Samaria and Gaza. 


[Question] Mr Eban, how, in actual fact, does one take lessons in modesty and soul 
searching? 


[Answer] Yes, what were the errors? The Israeli errors have been under the spotlights 
all the time: the acts of settlement and archeology which sullied the good atmosphere 
created in Ismailia and the great obstacles of giving Resolution 242 an interpretation 
which has no possibility of being accepted seriously by any country in the world and 
also giving what was possibly an exaggerated impression of the degree of backing which 
the peace program received from the United States and Britain at the beginning of the 
process. However, there have also been U.S, mistakes, mainly that the United States 
has not made and is not making a convincing attempt to get Egypt to take up the 
negotiations specifically and seriously concerning the problem o1 security and borders. 


As-Sadat is apparently sunk in the illusion that the fact of his recognition of the 
legitimacy of Israel's existence solves the problem of security. It may very well be 
that some Israeli statements in the past contributed to the illusion, when it was said 
that the heart of the problem was only the Arabs! refusal to recognize our legitimate 
existence. This gives a mistaken impression that a solution of the problem of 
legitimacy automaticelly solves the problem of security, and this is not so. 


Even after solving the problem of legitimacy, there is the problem of security, border 
adjustments, buffer zones, balances of forces, and As-Sadat is refusing to enter into 
[a discussion of] this, Let us assume that he has solved 70 percent of the problem by 
his recognition of us. What of the remaining 30 percent? What is the advantage of a 
bridge only 70 percent erected? The United States is not making an effort to convince 
As-Sadat of the need to moderate his positions, The effort the United States is 
directing at Israel is understandable but not balanced.

It is a fact that the United States stresses that, were Israel to accept at this stage 
the principle of the validity of 242 without defining the scope of the concession, then 
the United States would breath a sign of relief and might, perhaps, work more conscien- 
tiously toward renewing the momentum of negotiations. Therefore, the distinction between 
the Alinement and the coalition is no small thing--perhaps before November it was a 
matter of image and propaganda--now it is something operative and I believe that public 
opinion and the Knesset will have to decide on this matter. 


[Question] As for defining the reality in which we find ourselves at present, for 
example, yesterday, Foreign Minister Moshe Dayan said we have known more difficult 
times than these in the past. We had crises in 1956, for example. How do you view this? 


[Answer] You are picking at my memory because then I was responsible for conducting 
the negotiaticns. I hope that the foreign minister's equation is balanced, because 

in 1956-1957 it was clear that despite the severity of the tension the path to coopera- 
tion between the United States and Ben-Gurion would be found again. The United States 
wanted Israel's withdrawal, Israel wanted freedom of shipping and an international 
presence in Gaza and in the end each got what it wanted--the United States obtained our 
withdrawal; we, after all, got freedom of shipping in the Straits of Tiran and also a 
UN army that prevented attacks from Gaza. That is to say, at that time it was clear 
that Ben-Gurion would be able to achieve a rapprochement and compromise with the United 
States. The question that worries me=--and, it appears, our friends in the world--is 
will Mr Begin understand that, with all his natural loyalty to the principles of his 
peace plan, he must make an additional effort on the central issue that I have men- 
tioned? Whoever says, this is my plan and I refuse to change, develop or violate it, 
it is sanctified, you once welcomed it, welcome it again--I hope that he understands 
that in our present situation a further intellectual and political effort is required. 


[Question] Mr Eban, our time is short. Therefore, in one sentence--a national peace 
government--or as the prime minister defined it, a rational unity government. Are you 


in favor or against? 


[Answer] No, I understand Weizman's mood as well as the interest tnat the initiative 
aroused. However, in my opinion, preference should be given to cnanging the policy and 
not necessarily changing the composition cf the government. I'll put it this way: 

Were Mr Begin's government to adopt a reasonable position--242, a willingness in prin- 
ciple to make certain concessions in Judaea and Samaria--then I would propose not 
raising the issue of the composition of the government within the present parliamentary 
reality. I hope that the Labor Party--having the example of the DMC [Democratic Move- 
ment for Change], which hoped that by entering the government it would change policy, 
staring it in the face--I hope that the Labor movement decides, if such an appeal is 
made to it, to say, first of all, let's talk about policy, and in accordance with 

what is decided with respect to policy, we will decide on the composition. To speak 
about composition before speaking about policy is a case of putting the cart before 


the horse.
