"Morocco, which so far has always managed to alter the Mauritanian domestic 
situation to ite advantage, has no interest in such a development.” 4 senior 
official explained to me. in the eyes of others Rabat was seeking through 














its intransigence to push Nouakchott into the arms of the adversary in order 
to secure, through a kind of boomerang effect, the support of Paris. The 
French declaration cf support of Mauritania frustrated such planning, it is 
said in Nouakchott, Putting up a good front in the face of this setback, the 
Moroccan authorities have claimed to be “delighted” at this declaration. 


It is certain that Paris would not have greeted the establishment of a Tripoli- 
Algiers-Nouakchott axis with much enthusiasm. President Valery Giscard 
d'Estaing was kept closely posted on the negotiations between Mauritania and 
the POLISARIO Front. Two days before the signature of the Algiers agreement 
he saw Ahmedou Ould Abdallah, Mauritanian minister of foreign affairs, in 
Paris. In July 1979 a French plane had very discreetly flown Rene Journiac, 
the French president's adviser for African affairs, to Nouakchott. Paris 

has never concealed the fact that it has always wished to see a Mauritania 
simultaneously serving as a buffer zone between white Africa and Black Africa 
and strategically balancing the region by opposing both the Moroccan myths 
and the Algerian sirens. France even seems ready today to place at the dis- 
posal of Nouakchott the military advisers it has requested for the defense 

of Mauritanian territory. Will the parties move to the point of envisioning 
a defense agreement? 


This new situation provides a respite to Prime Minister Haidalla's government 
which will at long last be able to devote itself to postwar problems. In fact, 
in Nouakchott the atmosphere in August 1979 strangely resembled that of August 
1978, immediately following the coup d‘etat--with one less burden, that of the 
war. Both conservatives and progressives are polishing their weapons--peace- 
fully, naturally--to try to influence the government's policy. On 13 August 
1979, for the second time in a year, the representatives of the Confederation 
of Employers, all meeting behind the peace banner, paraded, some in Mercedes 
automobiles, some in pickup trucks, in the streets of Nouakchott to evidence 
their support of the Algiers agreement. Ten days later nearly 5,000 individ- 
uals hurried to the stadium in Nouakchott carrying streamers demanding the 
solution of the “national problem" and that of the feudal system. 


One can bet a lot that the neutrality observed in foreign policy will be 
translated in domestic policy by the search for the greatest common denomin- 
ator making it possible to govern. Each one is convinced here that "three 
governments this year is two governments too many." Reality is stubborn and 
economic problems are tenacious. The Mauritanian Treasury has funds to 

tide it over until November 1979, It will subsequently be necessary to make 
up for the shortfall to pay an excessively large public administration (130 
million ouguiya a month) and reimburse the foreign debt (U.S. $30 million) 
while the budgetary reciepts for 1979 are estimated at 6 billion ouguiya. At 
the end of August 1979 the minister of foreign affairs flew to Riyadh and a 
sizable Mauritanian delegation participated at the festivities marking the 
anniversary of the Libyan revolution in Tripoli. 


Reserved, secretive, he is a “man of the desert," it is said of the Number 
One Mauritanian, Lt Col Mohamed Khouna Ould Haidalla, who for the time being

where discussion and palaver under a tent around three small glasses of tea

of speculation on the political opinions of the head of government and, in the

For the time being Lt Col Haidalla is advocating especially efficiency and 
austerity. The operating expenses of the budget have been cut radically and 
the drive against those who fail to pay their taxes is on, With a rigor that 
is completely military, Haidalla has succeeded in 3 months in putting the 
officials back in their offices, Even ministers are not immune from Haidalla's 
surprise visits at 0730 hours in the morning. And for this to happen in 
Nouakchott is also a revolution. This is an upheaval that is nearly as inm- 
portant as the rupture of Mauritania's “umbilical cord” with Morocco.
