This rapid Lebanese diplomatic move wae an indication of the impact the 
interactions on the shelling will have on the domestic situation, particu- 
larly as the city of Sidon is considered the capital of the south and the 
last line of defense in the confrontation with Israel. 


However, this was not the only reason the state moved diplomatically to 
deal with the emerging military situation in the south. There were, in 
fact, several reasons, primarily the fact that the city of Sidon had the 
largest population of Sunnis after Beirut and Tripoli. 


The state was afraid that the Israeli shelling would stir up feelings and 
sensitivities in various areas of Lebanon, particularly Beirut. 


In this sphere, the Sunnis are considered the major underpinnings for the 
stability of the political situation in the country. Israel's venturing 
to shell the areas of this denomination would further complicate the 
situation, particularly if the state did not move to salvage the situa- 
tion and contain its aftereffects. Therefore, the Lebanese diplomatic 
machinery moved quickly, because it could not bear this political compli- 
cation at a time when it had demonstrated its failure and inability to 
bear the burden of the huge material and human destruction inflicted on 
Shi'ite cities and villages in the south, such as Tyre and al-Nabatiyah. 


Informed sources close to the foreign ministry say that the authorities 
decided to move, before it was too late, to defeat Israel's plan to stir 
up domestic unrest at a time when Lebanon was preparing for the battle 
of national entente. 


The sources add that the authorities quickly understood the Israeli plan 
and bent their efforts on stopping the shelling through intensive contacts 
with Washington, New York and the American embassy in Beirut.
