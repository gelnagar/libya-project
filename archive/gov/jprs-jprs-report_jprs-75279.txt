It would nevertheless be hasty to conclude from the above that the Gulf 
will remain a haven of peace in a troubled world and that oil will continue 
to flow from the region without interruption. 


First, the Palestinians are becoming increasingly dissatisfied as a result 
of recent developments and they are afraid that their cause may be sub- 
merged by regional strategic considerations. Most of all, they fear a 
tacit alliance between the Gulf states and the United States, resulting 
from the fears of the former of confrontation with the USSR. The Gulf 
countries might feel that the only way they can stand up to the Soviet 
Union is to seek guarantees from the United States, for which they would 
be willing to offer support on other fundamental issues. 


Should this occur, the Palestinians would be left with only one effective 
ally in the Arab world, Syria, and only one major international supporter, 
the Soviet Union. They clearly cannot accept the risk of isolation which 
this entails and will do anything possible to avert it. 


The best option open to the Palestinians is to ensure that interest in 
their cause focusses on the identification of Palestinian rights with the 
availability of Arab oil. Im the past, the oil weapon has been brandished 
but never really used, even during the embargo of 1973. Libya continued 
shipping oil to the United States at that time and crude from the Arab 
countries of the Gulf reached the United States through Okinawa. It was 
revealed subsequently that there was no serious icterruption of American 
supplies. 


The situation today is drastically different. In 1973, the Arabs had 
made some progress on the military front and were, in principle at least, 
unanimously ranged against Israei. Today, Egypt is no longer against 
Isreal, even if it is not actively allied with it. In the meantime, the 
Palestinians have lost enormous human and material resources as a result 
of the civil war in Lebanon and as the 1970s came to a close, they were 
at loggerheads with Libya. 


The Soviet invasion of Afghanistan threatens the Palestinians with isola- 
tion as it has strengthened rapprochement between Gulf states and already 
created an invisible alliance between them and U.S. attitudes if not 
formally with the government in Washington. 


The Palestinians can be expected to try to identify their cause even closer 
with the availability of Arab oil or to go for bust. And in the latter 
event, oil routes and installations could suffer devastating damage. The 
scales have tipped too far on one side and a time~bomb is ticking away. 
Perhaps some Arab Gulf leaders will heed this danger and take steps to 
avert the consequences. But as yet there is no sign that there they are 
aware of it.

His discussions with his visitors are never the same, and cover a wide range 

of subjects. He talks cautiously about hot domestic issues such as the 
agricultural revolution or the Western Sahara. He tried not to criticize 

the present or previous regime, but when he does, he tries to direct his 
criticism at a specific point or person. Politically speaking, his visitors 
are divided into leftist factions historically associated with him, and rightist 
groups who now think in terms of rallying behind him. The latter would try to 
take advantage of Ben Bella's emotional experience in prison and the current 
atmosphere created by Khomeyni's revolution to induce Ben Bella to assume a 
religious posture. 


Ben Bella's favorite subject is democracy, but he adapts it to Algeria's 
particular circumstances. He calls for popular councils, and has reservations 
about the parliamentary system, because, as he sees it, it gives the bourgeoisie 
an opportunity to increase its wealth and strengthen its political influence. 
The same goes for the multi-party system. He has reservations about it as well, 
and calls for rebuilding the FLN on new and better foundations. 


On this particular matter, Ben Bella insists that what he calls for is different 
from what ie taking place in Libya. He underscored this point as if he were 
afraid to be accused of being influenced by Qadhdhafi's ideas! On one occasion, 
a young intellectual accused him of having been a dictator in one way or 
another during his rule. An aroused Ben Bella replied: "I was accused of 
being [socialist], not dictatorial.--Who was dictatorial? Myself, or our 

late brother (meaning Boumedienne)?" Ben Bella then went into some self- 
criticism. He acknowledg,ed that many things happened about which he was not 
pleased. Question: "But how? Does that mean that you were powerless?" 
Answer: "Yes, in one way or another. I knew that some members of the govern- 
ment were against the revolution and the people. I was waiting for the 
opportunity to remove them. But up to [the time of the coup], I was unable 
to."

President Chadli Bendjedid's warning goes beyond the student category alone. 
It is intended for “the elements who have abused youth's enthusiasm, its

moment had come to make a choice between those who have faith in the social- 
ist option and in the revolution and those who do not believe in it and 
rather work against it." He made an appeal for mobilization and denounced 
the persons who, “adopting a position of expectation, seem to await the 
resurrection of the Messiah.” And he concluded, "To those, I will say: 

Let those who do not believe in our option know that Algeria is rich in 

men ready to serve and to build the country."
