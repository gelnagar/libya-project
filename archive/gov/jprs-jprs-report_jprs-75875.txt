{Article by Jussi Jalkanen: “Finnish Construction Firms Place Trust in 
International Cooperation") 


{Text) More and more frequently agreements are struck in the name of coop- 
eration in the area of economic activities. Finnish constructors also be- 
lieving in international cooperation have scampered away like lemmings 
around the world to secure their positions. 


“In cooperative talks there is much that is irrelevant. Cooperation itself 
cannot be the goal -- it must have some clearly defined goal,” states Direc- 
tor Matti Nuutinen, who has gained experience with the Yleinen Insinooritoi- 
misto (YIT) [General Engineering Office) from their dealings with several 
foreign partners in the Near East. 


In the exporting of construction work cooperation is generally interpreted 
rather broadly. Graduate Engineer Kalle-Pekka Savelkoski of the Urakoitsi- 
jaliito (Contractors League) estimated that more than half of all export con- 
tracts are accomplished in some form of cooperation. This also includes 
projects constructed with so-called cheap labor in foreign countries. 


In an actual cooperative project, on the other hand, a Finnish firm works on 
@ contract together with a foreign firm. In such a case a construction com 
pany and a supplier of equipment or two or more construction companies 
enter into an association. 





In the last decade Finland's exports in construction projects have increased 
at an explosive rate and amounted to more than 10 percent of domestic con- 
struction. This year it is estimated that construction work abroad will 
amount to 2.4 billion markkas and 17 countries will play host to Finnish 
construction companies. This growth, which was spurred on with great en- 
thusiasm, has now slacked off and generally the industry is now preparing 
for a slight reduction. 


Cooperation by Finnish constructors with foreign partners in third-party 
countries is still in the infant stage. In Iraq Vise and Lohja are con-

firm. YIT ie constructing water management projects in Medina and Riyadh

with a French partner and a fodder plant in the same area with the Swiss 
Buhler Firm. In addition to this, Vesi-Pekka is cooperating with the Bul- 
qarians in Libya. 


Cooperation Reduces Risks 


YIT's Nuutinen divides the most important aspects of cooperation into four 
groups: 


"The procurement of additional resources is a significant reason. In this 
way the economic base can be broadened, specialized knowledge, familiarity 
with local conditions, and so on can be obtained. 


“Enterprise risks can be reduced with cooperation. It makes no sense tha 
all the resources of a corporation should be tied up in some multibillion 
markka venture. Cooperation can be a kind of life insurance. 


“By using cheap labor we can improve our competitive ability. 


“In knowing our competitor we can propose cooperation and enter into an 
association. The interests are frequently different and a division of 
labor can be found.” 


Nuutinen states that none of these reasons can predominate over another. 
Each project is different. 


The YIT does not have a s-ecial network with respect to finding partners. 
New relationships can be found in the most varied manner. 


“In Medina we took the initiative. We had built several maintenance and 
training facilities and more were in the offing. We were looking for a part- 
ner who had an Islamic work force that could work in prohibited areas. Ccc 
had such a work force and the experience. 


"Since the cooperation went well, we expanded it to include water manage-

consider the establishment of a local corporation,” states Nuutinen.

Paris DEMAIN L'AFRIQUE in French 7 Apr 80 pp 23-33 
[Article by Roland Malet] 


[Text | year which has just begun is perhaps a crucial diplomatic year 
for the fighters of the POLISARIO Front. There is some chance that this 
very year a majority will see its way clear, between now and July, within 
the OAU to decide to admit the Saharan Arab Democratic Republic (SADR) as an 
integral separate member. If there are not enough votes, it will still be 
close, and the lost opportunity will certainly not reoccur in 1981. 


With the accession of Zimbabwe to the independence, the Saharan state is 
virtually certain to be recognized by Salisbury. Furthermore, there is no 
indication that Tripoli, which for a long time has been rendering substantial 
material and military aid to the struggle of the Saharan people, will not 
follow the same route. Up to now the Socialist People's Libyan Arab 
Jamahiriya has taken care to manage Rabat and avoid the compromise of 
Libyan-Moroccan diplomatic relations. But the events of Gafsa seem to have 
convinced President Mu'ammar Qadhdhafi that the obliging attitude he had 
taken toward King Hassan Il was not paying. The Sherifian sovereign had 
immediately espoused the thesis of the invasion of a portion of Tunisian 
territory by forces coming from Libya and, as a result, had sent elements of 
his own army as reinforcements. At this point “the spell was broken." 





Other African countries, which up to now had restricted themselves to a position 
of cautious reserve, now display an impending evolution of their positions 

on the conflict in the Western Sahara. Some no longer hide the sympathy 
inspired by the drumbeats of the armed struggle of the POLISARIO Front Army 

on the ground, going from one success to another and only rarely suffering 

a reverse. This is true of Mali, Mauritania, Niger, and Chad. 


Bamako, Nouakchott, Niamey and Ndjamena have just come out publicly in favor 
of the Saharans. They, of course, formulate their opinion more diplomatically, 
but it is the same in substance. In order to show their preference in this 
prickly business, they took advantage of the occasion of the summit meeting 

of Saharan states last 8 and 9 March in Bamako.

One could not have found more favorable circumstances. Five presidents of 
republics and a prime minister met in the Malian capital for the purpose of 
settling the details of the measures needed to strengthen cooperation and 
solidarity among their countries. Present were; the Algerian, Chadli 
Benjedid; the Malian, Moussa Traore; the Mauritanian, Mohammed Khouna Ould 
Haidalla; the Nigerian, Seyni Kountche; the Chadian, Goukouni Oueddet; and 
the Libyan, Abdesselam Jalloud, 


After expressing their "concern over the worsening situation prevailing in

Saharan people "to exercise their right to self-determination,” aud to this
