Summary (AI): A December 1981 CIA Office of Security memo reviews a New York Times story reporting that a Libyan-trained five-person team was reported to plan to kill President Reagan, and attaches press clippings (NYT, Newsweek, Washington Post) on Libyan assassination threats, Qaddafi's economic troubles and U.S. countermeasures. Nothing specific to Tripoli sites is described.
• Newsweek (Nov. 1981) reported Qaddafi's hit list reportedly included Vice President Bush, Secretary Haig and Secretary Weinberger.
• Newsweek reported Libya's oil production fell from 1.7 million to 600,000 barrels per day amid the oil glut; Ray Wilkinson reported from Tripoli.
• Reported Libyan expeditionary force in Chad of 5,000-10,000 men was withdrawn; about 1,000 killed or wounded.
• Washington Post reported Secret Service protection ordered for Baker, Meese and Deaver.
• 1981-12-03: Reported Libyan-trained five-person assassination team entered the United States to plan to kill President Reagan (NYT report, Dec. 3, 1981)
• 1981-10: Alleged Libyan assassination plot against U.S. Ambassador Maxwell Rabb in Italy, reportedly foiled

Libya’s Growing Woes 

In Tripoli last week, NEWSWEEK’s Nairobi bureau chief Ray 
Wilkinson assessed Muammar Kaddafi’s vulnerabilities in his 
own country: . 

Just a few months ago Kaddafi appeared untroubled—and 
untouchable. Oil revenues of $25 billion enabled him to pay for 
his program of domestic reform, to back his terrorist adventures 
beyond Libya and to thumb his nose at Ronald Reagan. No 
longer. The world oil glut has cut deeply into Libya’s surplus 
revenues. Foreign oil companies are turning feisty. Reagan 
keeps stepping up the pressure. 
And even within LibyaI saw some 
tentative but visible signs of oppo- 
sition to Kaddafi. “Right now he 
has just caught a cold,” one old 
hand in Tripoli told me. “But he 
will have to tread warily if he 
‘wants to avoid pneumonia.” 

The immediate problem for 
Kaddaftis oil. As the glut engulfed 
world markets, Tripoli’s produc- 
tion dropped by two-thirds, from 
1.7 million barrels to 600,000 bar-

$12 billion. Faced with Kaddafi’s

to drop Libya’s price by $1 a bar-

“The companies’ reaction has been, ‘Go tohell and stay there’, :

Cutbacks: Kaddafi’s oil-revenue crisis seems to be andermin- 
ing his ability to make mischief. He has begun to cut back aid to 
some of the fringe guerrilla and revolutionary groups around the 
world that he funded in the past. His cash-flow problem may 
have been a significant factor in his recent decision to with- 

draw his 5,000- to 10,000-man expeditionary force from Chad. 

The yearlong operation in Chad cost Kaddafi between $2 billion 
and $7 billion. Roughly 1,000 soldiers were killed or wounded, 

and the bodies of many were returned to their families with no 
explanation except a pinned note: ‘Died in the accomplishment

schemes, electricity projects, light industry and consumer im- 
ports. The cutbacks wil! not sit easily with a population that has

perceive in Kaddafi a new yearning to change his image as the
