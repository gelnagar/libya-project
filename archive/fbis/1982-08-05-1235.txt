LIBYA

JANA: U.S. HAD PRIOR KNOWLEDGE OF ISRAELI ATTACK 


LDO51050 Tripoli JANA in Arabic 1020 GMT 5 Aug 82 


[Text] Beirut, 5 Aug (JANA) -- The JANA correspondent in Beirut has learned that the 
U.S. administration had prior knowledge of the wide Zionist attack on West Beirut and 
the southern suburb yesterday. An informed political source in Beirut told our corre- 
spondent that the French ambassador to Lebanon on Wednesday evening a few hours before 
the start of the attack requested a meeting with President Sarkis of Lebanon and informed 
him of the enemy's intention to carry out a wide attack on all axes. The source added 
that President Sarkis met immediately with the U.S. envoy, informed him of the French 
report and asked him to inform Washington of the situation. These factors come at a 
time when the U.S. administration denies knowledge of the bestial wide Zionist attack 
on Beirut and its southern suburb yesterday.

[Excerpts] Reports from Jidda say the Yemeni presidents are making a tour of Arab countries, 
which is said to be part of an initiative to close Arab ranks to confront the Zionist 
invasion of Lebanon. 


The JANA political commentator says the pigs of the Arabian Peninsula have confirmed that 
they are the true enemy of the Arab nation even though they speak Arabic and have Arabic 
names. In reality, they have nothing to link them to Arabism. They are just like the 
Zionists and the Americans. 


The hireling, hysterical regime in Riyadh is using blackmail to impose oppressive custodian- 
ship over all pan-Arab issues. Through this exposed attitude, they have made links with 

the rightwing Phalangists. Behind the scenes in Riyadh, they have struck a close relation- 
ship with Bashir al-Jumayyil to stab the Palestinian resistance in the back. All indica- 
tions confirm this evil course of the Saudi regime. This regime previously supplied the 
Phalangist rightwing with weapons and war equipment to enable it to carry out its criminal 
task. 


The Saudi regime by all standards intends to contain the most honorable and noble cause. 
The Saudi regime openly practices all forms of treason and subservience. For this reason, 
the Saudi regime should not be trusted or contacted. 


The JANA political commentator concluded: The stands of the Saudi ruling family toward 
the pan-Arab cause are neither patriotic nor pan-Arab. They all lead to treason and 
capitulation. 


AZ-ZAHF AL-AKHDAR ON PRESERVING REVOLUTION 


PM291621 London AZ-ZAHF AL-AKHDAR in English 23 Jul 82 p 7 


[Ahmad Ibrahim article: "Should We Revolutionize the Revolution?"] 


[Text] If it is possible to revolutionise the state, shouldn't we seriously start thinking 
about nationalising the revolution? That is, should we first transform the revolution into 
a state -- a revolutionary state, of course? 


If it is advisable to proceed in that manner, then what are the steps that need to be taken? 
If we find it difficult to transform the state into the revolution and reshape its security 
forces into an effective force which aids and upholds the revolution, then surely we must 
give serious consideration to the alternative. 


And that alternative would, of course, be to transform the revolution into state, a state 
with established institutions especially designed to serve the aims of the revolution. 
Examples of such transformatory attempts are certainly not lacking in the historiography 
of countries where revolutions have taken place. 


There are two opposing sides to such a proposition. One can either make the revolutionary 
aims the basis for the state and its political and economical institutions, or else one 
can ignore attempting the above and concentrate on the practical application of the 
revolutionary aims. We could thus try to acquire and establish new social codes as a firm 
basis for interaction. 


If we decide to proceed along the first of these paths, what we would in effect do, is to 
adopt the traditional image of the state -- though we would dress it in our own (revolu- 
tionary) colours. It would mean chenging the essence of something, but not its form, 
however desirable such a complete change would be.

A Complete and Comprehensive Change 


But if we were to follow the second of the paths outlined above, we would carry out, without 
hindrance, a complete and comprehensive change. We would not only establish our revolution- 
ary aims, but we would do so by inventing specific and suitable new forms without being 
hampered by the traditional shape of the institutions of the old nation-state, whether 

they be in the political, economic, social or any other spheres. 


When a revolution means the release of all capabilities and energies in the service of the 
creation of a new world, genuinely free from tyranny, backwardness, disease and ignorance, 
then the old world is bound to collapse. 


Then a truly new life dawns for man, enabling him to live the revolution practically and 
subjectively to the full, rather than simply dwelling on it and being smitten by its 
beauty or blinded by its glare. 


Of course. the most difficult thing to achieve is that total release of all the harnessed 
and suppressed energies and forces. What is most certain is that such release cannot be 
achieved within the framework of the old-type state whose institutions were created in the 
first place precisely to prevent any such explosion. 


In such states all actions are governed by one-sided sets of laws, especially designed to 
enslave the weak and strengthen the strong. Laws then are the basis on which such nations 
exist and most of the abilities and forces of society must be kept in check for the benefit 
of the legislators. 


It thus becomes obvious that the building of a revolutionary structure in a state set up 
according to traditional paradigms cannot possibly be achieved, except as a sterile 
intellectual exercise. From a practical point of view a "revolutionary state" is a 
contradiction in terms and the word revolution loses all meaning when institutionalised by 
state structures. But the question remains: why do we even think of transforming the 
revolution into a state? 


Revolutionaries try to give their values concrete shape by creating new political, economic 
and social settings suitable to their aims. What they often do is essentially to try to 
transform the revolution itself into a state, forgetting that in their naive attempt to 
build such a state they necessarily abandon what was most progressive and innovative in 
their revolutionary thinking. 


This is so because of necessity. The state they are trying to establish can bear no 
relation to their primordial aims and values. Or if, for argument's sake, we grant the 
possibility of such a metamorphosis, then we have to realise that for such a revolutionary 
state to come about, much time has to pass. 


It would certainly take very long for those revolutionary aims and values to ripen and 
mature to the extent that they could produce a completely new revolutionary philosophy 
which would totally imbibe society as a whole; only after that has taken place could one 
think of evolving a revolutionary state that would incorporate the early aims and values. 


A national revolution, such as the Iranian revolution, is an excellent illustration of our 
point. The people who rose against the oppressive royal society consisted of millions of 
unarmed Iranians who achieved success because of their strong faith. But it was not long 
before we saw this revolution change into a state with a parliamentary system, headed by 

a president who kep the power of government administration and the security forces firmly 
in his hands. 


Thus the people who toppled the shah ‘returned to their homes, their work and social 
structure without having achieved anything really new. Even though we cannot possibly 
compare the shah's oppressive regime with that of the Islamic revolution, we must point 
to the survival of the same state structure and institutions in post-revolutionary Iran.

Immunity in Iran is given exclusively to members of parliament, and governing is the 
exclusive sphere of the government and is carried out by ministers. 


The authority of the Iranian religious leaders is very similar to that exercised by the 
pope, who guides a number of religious states and not only the one of which he is the 
nominal head. The pope's special area is of course, religious affairs and he legislates 
from this stance on all matters of social life. He is aided in this by a religious body 
known as the Ss. congresso per il clero, the sacred congregation for the clergy. 


We saw at the same time and had it confirmed by the Iranians themselves that the president 
of Iran and members of the Iranian Parliament betrayed the people. Furthermore, extensive 
liquidations took place among the ranks of the army as well as among high officials, 
starting with the prime minister and some of his ministers. 


Some of those executec had been major spokesmen for the Iranian revolution. This is a 
clear example of the pitfalls which await a revolution that tries to build a state on top 
of the corrupt structures of the ancient regime. 


What took place in Iran is less serious than what is currently taking place in Libya in 
terms of the opposition between the revolution and the state it built. What helped Iran, 
by giving the revolution the necessary clout, were the revolutionary guards who were armed 
and decided to use those arms to impose their will upon the government, thus securing its 
ultimate involvement. 


In Libya, the role of the revolutionary committees is restricted to the theoretical 
spreading and teaching of the revolution's aims and principles and to similar critical 
stands against the government. Thus the government departments remain free and unrestricted 
to take the measures they choose, measures often hostile to the revolution. 


A Libyan Example 


A newspaper such as AZ-ZAHF AL-AKHDAR reduced its print run and its distribution by 50 
percent, forced to do so by a decision of the general national committee. This is an 
administration committee with no connection with the spreading and teaching of the 
revolution. 


Its members were simply asked to decide upon an economic plan that would reduce excess 
expenditure and non-essentials. Among its ‘economies’ was the measure that restricted 
the overseas distribution of the revolutionary committees’ news organ. I could easily 
cite another dozen examples showing the inevitable clash between the revolution and the 
state, and the basic conflict between those two, a conflict which makes it impossible to 
reconcile the interests of both. 


Even those committed revolutionaries which we pick to fill administrative posts in govern- 
ment soon become part of the government and, as such, small cogs in an enormous machine. 
They carry out what is required of them without delay or questioning. At the same time 
they consider any honest attempt to stand up to this machine as an attack on their person. 


In this way the governmental 'machine' takes away from us even the most actively committed 
revolutionaries through whom we had hoped to control our revolutionary government and force 
it into the direction most beneficial for the achievement of a truly revolutionary society.

Even worse than simply alienating our revolutionary members through working for the 
governmental machinery is the fact that the governmental administration, through those 
same co-opted revolutionaries, infiltrates our ranks and recruits ‘sympathetic’ groups 
from within the main body of the revolution. 


Non-revolutionary elements use that situation to state their opposition and their lack of 
respect even for the national committee and its decisions. Thus while declaring their 
contempt for the revolution, they rely for their protection upon the traditional forces of 
the government and its institutions, which are headed by the army. 


We often hear that the revolution survives thanks to the army. In fact, the existence of 
the army means the continuation of disease in society and the survival of the remaining 
traditional government institutions. 


Many of those who are retrogressive and entrenched within these government departments 
continously foil any revolutionary change and rob the revolution of its progressive 
content. This points out the shameful weakness prevailing in the revolutionary organisa- 
tions. 


We know that the forces of imperialism and the local reactionary movements are betting on 
the liquidation of the Islamic revolution in Iran by the army. Their expressed hope is 

that as soon as the army comes back from the war in the south, it will oppose the revolution 
with force! 


A Danger for the Revolution 


We also know that the imperialist and reactionary forces have previously used the army 
against the national revolution in Libya. Its failure at that time does not mean that 
that danger has vanished for good. 


All this should make it absolutely clear that the known establishments and institutions of 
the state constitute a direct physical and ideological danger to the revolution. Hence 
the nationalisation of the revolution is an open attempt at aborting it and reversing its 
course. 


That is why revolutionaries have to guard against all such attempts and work at destroying 
the state relentlessly, so that the people will retain their power over that state. In 
strengthening the revolutionary organisations, the people themselves will be strengthened 
and help to root the aims and values of the revolution ever more deeply. 


Nor should one forget to regularly pull out the fangs and claws of the non-revolutionary 
elements. However tough revolutionaries may be, they will never be as cruel as those who 
have decided to abort the revolution and the power of the people. 


It is thus justified for revolutionaries to use whatever violence is necessary against 
the traditional format of society and the state. The structures which presently prevail 
in the world have no purpose but to reduce man to the level of a biological animal which 
only eats to multiply. When revolutionaries wish to restore the dignity of man and to 
build a world worthy for him to live in, they chose a lonely path to reach their goal. 


A path which is made even more difficult by the complex and savage methods used by oppres- 
sive governments everywhere in the world. These methods are not only those used in 
Palestine and El Salvador, they also include those steps taken to corrupt the revolution 
from the inside, as shown by the attempts of the bourgeoisie in Iran, and in Libya by the 
level of corruption, as well as by the various coup attempts. 


To try and retain any part of the repressive structures is a regrettable betrayal and a 
stand which does not emanate from a correct analysis of the situation or from an enlightened 
examination of the question at hand.
