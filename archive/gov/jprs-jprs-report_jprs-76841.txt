/Text/ In Paris in 1961, bombs were exploding before everyone who called for 
Algerian independence, The Boulevard of the Champs Elysees was full of troops and 
armored vehicles, as it was on the eve of the German invasion, in the fear that 
paratroops and generals who wanted to overthrow DeGaulle before he agreed to inde- 
pendence would strike; the newspapers--all the newspapers--were talking about the 
Arabe with resentment; and the Arabs coming to France could be numbered on the fing- 
ers of one's hand. 





Paris was drenched in the blood of Algerians and it was bearing--and still does 
bear--the effects of the Suez war and Bizerte on its face. French relations with 
the eastern countries, eastern countries of all kinds, were tense or broken. There 
were almost daily demonstrations against the french on the streets of Damascus, 
Beirut and Cairo. 


London's situation was not much better. There were the harbingers of revolution 

in Aden and there was the shadow of Suez and Eden's defeat in the canal. There 

was “abd-al-Nasir's daily war against “the English,” In brief, all Europe was non- 
existent in the Arab world. Conversely there was no Arab presence in Europe. There 
were no relations. West Germany was still giving Israel billions in aid in repara- 
tions for the war. 


In brief, “greater Europe, including Italy and the bitter memories it had left in 
Libya were still a symbol of a long stage of colonialism, the mandate, Sykes-Picot 
and the Balfour declaration.
