AL-QADHDHAFI ADVOCATES COOPERATION WITH SUDAN 


LD181824 Tripoli JANA in Arabic 1515 GMT 18 Feb 81 


[Text] Tripoli, 18 Feb (JANA)--The leader of the Great 1 September Revolution, Colonel 
Mu'ammar al-Qadhdhafi, received at noon today in Tripoli the brother ambassador of the 
Republic of the Sudan in the Socialist People's Libyan Arab Jamahiriyah and conveyed 
verbal cordial message to the Sudanese president, Ja'far Numayri, affirming the con- 
tinued consolidation of fraternal relations between the two peoples and two countries, 
and the preparedness of the Jamahiriyah to open new horizons for civil and military 
cooperation, such as participating in arming and organizing the Sudanese Army, helping 
in the reclamation of the virgin land, participating in the grain cultivation program 
and in absorbing surplus workers in the Sudan.

SAUDI PAPER INTERVIEWS BNEIJARA ON SAHARAN ISSUE 


GF180914 Riyadh AL-JAZIRAH in Arabic 17 Feb 81 p 10 


[Interview granted by Mauritanian Prime Minister Ahmad Ould Bneijara to AL-JAZIRAH in 
Mecca--date and place not given] 


[Excerpts] Question: The Afro-Arab dialogue has a long history. How can this dialogue 
be strengthened and increased to serve the interest of both sides? 


Answer: Afro-Arab cooperation must be built on firm and principled bases that support 
confrontation with the common enemy. In view of the geographical position of the Islamic 
Republic of Mauritania, its historical role in expanding Islamic teaching and the Arab- 
Islamic civilization in western Africa, Mauritania is doing its best to realize this 
closeness between the two worlds on the aforementioned bases. 


Question: What about your withdrawal from the conflict taking place in the Sahara? 
What is your current position? 


Answer: The brothers’ conflict in the Sahara is really painful to us. We were involved 
in this dispute but withdrew in order to stop shedding the blood of Arab and Muslim 
brothers. We also want to contribute to and crystallize a comprehensive settlement that 
will restore peace to the region and prepare for the creation of fraternal cooperation 
through the unity of the peoples of the large Arab western flank. 


We hope the brothers will reach a political settlement through dialogue and wisdom as a 
basis for determining legitimate rights. Our stand is total neutrality, because all the 
disputing parties are Arabs and Muslims who do not want to take more lives or create 

new problems for the Islamic world. 


Question: Does this mean you are going to recognize the establishment of a Saharan state 
if all the conflicting parties agree on this? 


Answer: As I have said, Mauritania was involved in this knotty problem and entered the 
war because of it. It was a war of attrition that depleted our resources and economy 
and diverted our attention from our internal affairs, development and prosperity. We 
realized that it was useless for us to continue with this war that creates new victims 
daily. With regard to the recognition of a Saharan state to which you refer in your 
question, we are opposed to having a black minority or other entities in Mauritania. We 
adhere to the stands of the OAU and the United Nations.

STRATEGIES, REASONING IN U.S. RELATIONS EXAMINED 


TA181500 Tel Aviv ‘AL HAMISHMAR in Hebrew 18 Feb 81 p 1 


[Political correspondent Dalya Shehori's report: "Begin to U.S. in April, Will Be Given 
"Low Profile’ Treatment"} 


[Text] President Reagan will invite Prime Minister Menahem Begin to visit Washington in 
mid-April. This is the date that is shaping up according to information received in 
Jerusalem. As-Sadat's visit, at the beginning of April, will precede Begin's. Sources 
in Jerusalem yesterday averred that the Reagan administration has no desire to help the 
Likud win a majority in the election, and therefore Begin will be received correctly but 
no more than that, and his visit will produce no notable achievements. According to this 
assessment, the United States' intention to accord Begin ‘low profile' treatment will 
dovetail with President as-Sadat's unwillingness to renew the autonomy negotiations. 


Against the background of the moderate expectations held out for Begin's forthcoming U.S. 
visit, no concrete political achievements are being hoped for from the talks that foreign 
Minister Yitzhaq Shamir is about to hold in Washington. It looks as if the only achieve- 
ment of this visit will be the procurement of aid to Israel in compensation for the ship- 
ment of offensive U.S. arms to Saudi Arabia, which the administration initiated even 
before Shamir's visit. 


It was noted yesterday in Jerusalem that the Reagan administration's agreement to supply 
Saudi Arabia with F-15's does not constitute the fulfillment of a Carter administration 
commitment, as the new administration is now saying. While the Carter administration did 
undertake to accede to the Saudi requests, the deal was frozen due to Israeli opposition. 
Now the freeze has been lifted due to the Reagan administration's desire to move toward 
Saudi Arabia. 


Sources in Jerusalem have expressed apprehension at this tendency. In their estimation, 
a prominently pro-Saudi tendency exists among the heads of the U.S. administration, 
deriving from oil interests which are disguised with political reasons--such as the need 
to help Saudi Arabia in order to bring about the formation of a bloc of moderate states 
under U.S. influence in the Middle East. 


The possibility of Israel's launching an open information campaign in the United States, 
and mobilizing senators and congressmen against any supply of U.S. arms to Saudi Arabia, 
was rejected by the cabinet for two reasons: 


(A) It was feared that Israel would not succeed in gaining a majority in the Congress for 
its position, thus exposing Israel's weakness. 

(B) Had an Israeli initiative in the Congress succeeded, Israel would have created ten- 
sion and a residue of bitterness toward it on the part of the Reagan administration, and 
this could be harmful for the Likud in the election campaign. 


It was therefore decided to employ only quiet information against the supply of arms to 
Saudi Arabia, in the knowledge that the prospect of changing the administration's aim in 


this manner is virtually nonexistent. 


HA'ARETZ COMMENTS ON U.S.-ISRAELI RELATIONS 


TA171524 Tel Aviv HA'ARETZ in Hebrew 17 Feb 81 p 1i 


(U.S. correspondent Gid'on Samet commentary: "The Dangers Inherent in the New Understand- 
ing" ] 


[Text] Under different circumstances, not in the dying days of the government, the visit 
to Washington by the foreign minister and the expected visit by the prime minister could 
have been considered very important. 


V. 419 Feb 81 Nz ISRAEL 


New winds are now blowing between the two capitals, some dangerous and some refreshing. 
The present period could have been particularly suitable for grabbing the bull by its 
horns; but it is to be feared that in more than one affair Israel may soon find itself 
caught on these horns. 

Some time ago I cited a comparison I heard in Washington between Presidents Carter and 
Reagan. The first continually contradicted himself in his attempt to be nice to all; 
the other, in his own way, offers a deal to the party that views itself harmed. It seems 
that the Reagan administration is offering a deal to Israel today. This deal has merits 
as well as dangers, but needs to be seriously considered. The special nature of the 
deal is that, contrary to similar situations on the political and commercial level, this 
deal is proposed to Israel exactly when its ability to influence events and pull strings 
in Washington is increasingly weakening. 


Two examples have clarified this fact lately. One was the Israeli Embassy's refraining 
from pursuing its traditional path of action in curbing the U.S. intention to sell the 
additional equipment for the F-15 planes possessed by Saudi Arabia. It is not only that 
Israel was not succeeded in its poor persuasion efforts, but it has also signalled to its 
friends in the Congress that they cannot expect to enlist support through traditional 
steps, such as the dispatch of senators’ letters to the administration. This approach, 
which is in itself a novelty in Israeli diplomatic practices in Washington, stemmed from 
the assessment that the chances for success are zero. The main reason for this does not 
lie in decreased support for Israeli interests in the present Congress, but in the 
changed circumstances in the Persian Gulf. A request by Saudi Arabia today cannot be 
considered in the same manner as that same request in 1978, when it was rejected. Israel 
has therefore not tried its muscles and the U.S. administration needed only a short time 
to realize Israel's weakness in the U.S. field. 


In his first meeting with Secretary of State Haig, Ambassador 'Evron left no room for 

mistake regarding Israel's weak bargaining position. Mr Haig indeed praised the manner 

in which Ambassador 'Evron presented the Israeli position on the supply of equipment to Riyadh, but 
beyond the diplomatic jargon, he received the message that was of particular interest to 

hin: Israel is ready to talk."" One could not interpret ‘Evron's remarks in any way other 
than as acquiescence to the Saudi deal, provided that this is coordinated with Israel and, 
especially, that Israel receives something suitable in return. It is clear that Saudi 

Arabia would have received its request even if Israel had raised idle threats. In any 

event, the interesting lesson, as mentioned, is that the U.S. administration is today 
inclined towards a deal with Israel, although the way is open for it to do as it pleases. 


This may seem alright. Israel is giving up arms which it does not possess in return for 
U.S. consultations, U.S. abstention from unilateral steps and an appropriate compensation 
in various fields. However, a week after the meeting--last week--it emerged that the 
first element, at least, has not come true. The U.S. administration, not saying a single 
word to the hopeful, good-wishing Israeli Embassy, informed the Congress of its intention 
to sell 24 Cobra helicopters to Jordan. 


The lesson has been learned: Israel has lost its muscle. Israel will do nothing anyway, 
so why provoke it and bait it with an advance announcement? It is better to surprise. 

This event indicates the dangers inherent in the "new understanding" between Israel and 

the Reagan administration. Israel has, in fact, already given up one of its most important 
assets while what it gets in return now depends on one element only: the U.S." good will 
and commitment to Israel's welfare and security. It is as though Washington said: We 

know where Israel stands. Let us talk about the price now. 


Israel's ambition now is to be "partner" and part of the U.S." strategic considerations 
in the Middle East. This was what Mr Haig could understand from Ambassador ‘Evron's 
remarks in their meeting. 


19 Feb 81 


The trouble, though, is that those considerations which now urge the U.S. administration 

to hurry to do what the Carter administration put off all the time--such as acceptance of 
almost every Saudi request--will be the ones that prevent the realization of the Israeli 

dream of real, not just verbal, strategic cooperation. 


The other danger is that the Israeli change practically invites a new arms race with Arab 
countries. The illusion that the scope of security expenditures could perhaps be slowed 
down after years of extensive procurement is now doomed for final shelving. 


This new understanding has advantages also, of course. The list of Israeli requests can 
be diversified. The basic deal--equipment to compensate for the imbalance caused by U.S. 
military supply of friendly Arab countries--is the simplest of all. Moreover, when the 
account books open and the hand--that of the administration that signs deals--keeps on 
signing, this can lead in a number of directions. One direction is financial: A re- 
arrangement of the system of huge debts owed to the United States by postponing the dates 
of repayment and converting some of the loans into grants. 


The other direction concerns the strategic field and this has financial implications. 

An idea heard here, (without the administration giving any sign of support for it) is to 
lease U.S. planes to Israel for a small fee. In addition to the economical aspect involved, 
the U.S. planes that will be stationed in various airports will be sort of a guarantee 
against an attack on what is, in fact, U.S. property. While Israel instinctively objects 

to the stationing of U.S. forces on its territory and the United States treats this 
possibility as a "mere hypothesis" (as stated by Secretary of Defense Weinberger), the 

new dialogue can lead to U.S. naval presence in Haifa. We have in fact already raised 

this possibility to the Americans. 


Other possible items on the Israeli list of requests are a U.S. consent to the sale of 
Israeli fighter planes worldwide and U.S. consent to coproduction with us, pressure for 
U.S. presence in the Sinai air fields after the final Israeli withdrawal and uncompromis- 
ing insistence on the Sinai supervision arrangements in general. All this is very 
valuable but it is very difficult today to know the chances for having this. At the 
moment, doubts are increasing, doubts concerning not only the basic reasons that will 
prevent the United States from meeting Israel halfway on various subjects but also the 
question whether the Israeli Government knows what it wants. Will the foreign minister 
and prime minister arrive in Washington with a clear idea about these far-reaching issues? 
Will they conduct intelligent and comprehensive discussions in the suitable forum? 


Besides, the Israeli-U.S. understanding cannot be restricted, in this period or in the 
coming years, to issues of armament, money and what Israel insists on calling "the 
strategic dialogue." The United States will want to know what the government thinks 
about the future of the peace process, the future of the West Bank and more significant 
Palestinian participation. It will not be too long before the United States will be 
urged by the Arabs to hear what we, nevertheless, have to say about the tightening of 
relations between Egypt and the PLO and about the signs that emerge here and there on 
possible tactica’ changes in the PLO's moves. 


One should wait and see if the government and Mr Shamir, who is leaving for Washington 
today, are prepared to discuss the implications of the new situation developing in 
Washington. What is clearer, though, is that the U.S. administration prefers to hold 
this discussion with the next government.

NISIM ATTACKS RECENT AS-SADAT, KREISKY STATEMENTS 


TA181714 Tel Aviv IDF Radio in Hebrew 1510 GMT 18 Feb 81 


[Text] The Government of Israel regards the latest remarks by as-Sadat on the establish- 
ment of a Palestinian government-in-exile as a contradiction of the Camp David accords, 
acting Foreign Minister Moshe Nisim said today from the Knesset rostrum. The minister 

was replying to motions to the Knesset agenda submitted by MK's Yosef Rom and Ge'ula 

Kohen on this issue. Among other things, Nisim attacked Austrian Chancellor Bruno Kreisky, 
describing him as nearly a sworn enemy of Israel. Our Knesset correspondent, Gil'ad 
Mishori, brings excerpts from minister Nisim's statements: 


[Begin recording] [Nisim] Anyone who suggests or raises the idea of a government-in- 

exile contradicts both the spirit and the letter of the Camp David agreements. The fact 
that the idea, first raised in 1972, is being raised again today, even though a drastic 
change has taken place since then--and that is the Camp David agreement, the peace treaty-- 
does not set well. 


{[Mishori] Minister Moshe Nisim repeated the statement that the responsibility for the 
stalemate in the autonomy talks lies with Egypt and asked what he called our friends in 
Egypt not to intervene in Israeli domestic affairs. In these matters we want none of 
your honey and none of your sting, he said. After such remarks, the minister then 
attacked Austrian Chancellor Bruno Kreisky for his statements in Cairo: 


[Nisim] It is difficult to accept what I would term such gall, almost verging on impudence, 
that a prime minister of a foreign country, who is hostile to the State of Israel--I 

would almost to as far as to say that he is a foe of Israel--permit himself to intervene 

in Israeli affairs. 


[Mishori] The continuation of the peace process is conditional on the adherence to the 
Camp David accords, the minister repeated, and anyone who would want to either distort 
them or amend them would not be contributing to the peace process. 


The issue was transferred to the Knesset Defense and Foreign Affairs committee by a 
majority vote, with Mapam and Sheli objecting. [end recording] 


"SOURCES' TERM AS-SADAT REMARKS ON PLO 'GRAVE' 


TA180832 Tel Aviv HA'ARETZ in Hebrew 18 Feb 81 pp l, 2 
{Political correspondent's report: "Government Sources: As-Sadat's Remarks Are Grave"] 


[Text] Government sources yesterday evening reacted harshly to remarks made in the past 
few days by President as-Sadat concerning the Middle East, including the role te is 
designating for the PLO to play in the peacemaking process in the region. The sources 
said that As-Sadat has recently made many remarks, some of them contradictory, but his 
ideas are grave and are unacceptable to Israel. The encouragement he is giving European 
governments to join the peacemaking process in the Middle East is not acceptable to 
Israel because of the Common Market countries’ adherence to the Venice declaration. 

The Egyptian president's suggestion that a Palestinian government-in-exile be formed was 
firmly rejected on 16 February by the prime minister. 


What As-Sadat said yesterday in favor of the PLO's being integrated into a Palestinian 
leadership is considered grave by government sources in Jerusalem, who note that all 
these statements are inconsistent with the Camp David accords. The sources add that it 
may be assumed that As-Sadat's remarks about the PLO were influenced by his talks with 
Austrian Chancellor Bruno Kreisky.

MOBILIZATION EXERCISE TO BE HELD ‘IN NEAR FUTURE’ 


TA190608 Jerusalem Domestic Service in Hebrew 0500 GMT 19 Feb 81 


[Text] A public callup exercise for reservists will be held in the near future. The 
exercise is designed to train the reservists in the method of the public, open callup, 
and to test the method's efficiency. The public callup will be based on the publication 
of units' mobilization codewords over radio and television. The exercise will involve 
several thousand reservists, as well as a small number of vehicle reserve units. 


When he hears the announcement on radio or television, or when he learns of the public 
callup, the reservist should check whether the codeword for his unit is included in the 
list. If his codeword has been broadcast or if it appears, he must report immediately 

to his permanent meeting place, or report directly to his unit. A reservist whose vehicle 
must also be brought in must first go to the permanent lot for the vehicle, and then 
proceed to his own unit. 


This exercise does not cancel or shorten the period of service for reservists who have 
been summoned for reserve service according to the annual plan by means of personal callup 
orders sent to them in advance. 


The public is requested to assist those reporting to their units on the day the exercise 
is held to get to their pickup points or to their units. This has been a prior announce- 
ment concerning a public callup exercise for reservists which will be held in the near 
future. 


POLL SHOWS MAJORITY SUPPORT WEST BANK SETTLEMENT 


TA171256 Jerusalem POST in English 17 Feb 81 p 3 


[Political correspondent Mark Segal report: "Jerusalem POST poll: 70 Percent of Public 
Supports Continuing Settlement"] 


[Text] Tel Aviv--A distinctly hawkish trend in public opinion emerges from the latest 
Jerusalem POST poll, with about 70 percent of the public favouring the continuation of 
settlement in the West Bank, in one form or another, and the extension of Israeli 
sovereignty over the Golan Heights. Likewise, the poll shows that most Israelis either 
favor the government's tough line of dealing with the inhabitants of the territories, or 
do not think it impedes the peace process. The above data emerged from a poll conducted 
by the Modi’in Ezrahi Applied Research Centre among 1,273 adults. 


Question: Do you think we should continue with Jewish settlement in Judea and Samaria? 


Answer: Yes (under certain conditions)--40.9 percent; yes in all parts--26.7 percent; 

yes, outside populated centres--2.4 percent; yes, close to 1967 lines--0.6 percent; 
no--26.3 percent; undecided--3.1 percent. Analysis of the data showed that the traditional 
Likud constituency is overwhelmingly for continued settlement, along with National 
Religious Party and Tehiya voters. 


Question: Should Israeli sovereignty be extended immediately over the Golan Heights? 


Answer: Yes--68.3 percent; no--26.6 percent; undecided--5.1 percent. The majority 
favouring the extension of Israeli sovereignty over the Golan Heights cuts across party 
lines and encompasses all socioeconomic strata and communities. 


Question: In your opinion does the tough line in the territories advance the cause of 
peace? 


Answer: Yes--25.7 percent; bears no influence on it--38.4 percent; impairs its progress 
--30.0 percent; undecided--5.9 percent. Those belonging to higher status groups, inclined 
to Labour and Shinu, thought that this policy was harmful to the cause of peace.
