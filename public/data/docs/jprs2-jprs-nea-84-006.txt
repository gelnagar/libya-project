ARAB LEAGUE PUBLISHES STATISTICS ON HUMAN RESOURCES 
Baghdad AL-THAWRAH in Arabic 4 Oct 83 p 4 
[Article by Badr Ghaylan: "Human Resources in the Arab Nation"] 

{Text] Just as the Arab nations differ in their geographic locations, and in 
the availability of natural resources, in the degree of wealth of some as op- 
posed to others, they differ in population count and, consequently, in the 
amount of labor available. 

According to the latest statistics, published by the Arab League in cooperation 
with a number of Arab organizations, the population of the Arab Nation, as of 
1982, was estimated at about 172 million compared with 167 million in 1981. 
This population is distributed very unevenly among the Arab nations, since we 
find that three~fifths of the Arab population live in only four of the nations, 
Egypt, Sudan, Algeria and Morocco, while Egypt alone contains about a quarter 
of the Arab World's population. Each of the other three nations contains 
between 11 and 12 percent. If we take into account the total land area of the 
Arab Nation, amounting to about 14 million square kms, we find that the general 
population density in the Arab Nation is about 12 persons per 1 square km. 

This density is about three times less than the world average, but despites its 
paucity, it still exceeds the national population density for nearly half the 
Arab states, since the average in many of them drops below five persons. There 
are three nations, Saudi Arabia, Libya and Mauritania, whose land area extends 
for about 5 million square kms, in which only 13 million persons live. In 
other words, they cover 36 percent of the total land area of the Arab Nation 
compared to 80 percent of its population. As a result, we find that there 

are two opposing groups of nations, one charrcterized by population exceeding 
its share of the land, while the other is exactly the opposite. 

Despite the increase in the percentage of population in the 15-65 age group, 
the percentage of workers among them is less than half. In addition to that, 
youth enters into the labor field in the majority of Arab countries, with ages 
ranging from 9 to 15. They hold jobs not to help them develop their mental 
faculties or their skills, but this phenomenon is attributable either to a 
shortfall of resources in specific countries with which to develop educational 
opportunities for young people, or they are remote from educational centers, 
or due to the fact the incomes of their providers are low, or in most cases, 
their poverty is what leads to increasing the percentage of support. This 

amounts to three times what it is in the advanced countries. The natural 
population growth in the Arab Nation ensures an annual increase fluctuating 
between 3.5 and 5 million persons. However, each country's share of this 
increase is relative to the importance of the local human population more than 
what the percentage of increase itself achieves. Egypt, which has about 25

The Arab work force in 1982 was estimated at about 51 million, increasing an- 
nually at a rate that fluctuates between 2.8 and 3 percent. That is, about 
1.5 million workers enter the labor market each year. However, the lack of 
homogeneity in distribution of the labor force, the growth disparity in the 
Arab states and the differences in pay scales and purchasing power all have 
led to an active emigration of labor forces, whether within the Arab Nation 
or abroad. This total force is estimated at about 7.5 million persons, i.e., 
about 14.7 percent of the total labor force, of whom 6 million workers are 
employed in the Arab nations and 1.5 million in foreign countries, in various 
specialities and technical and administrative professions. 

It is known that the majority of the transient labor force goes to the oil- 
producing Arab countries. This is helped by the expansion of employment op- 
portunities, the increase in wage rates and the negligible or non-existent 
taxes. The Arab Gulf states, including Iraq, attract workers from Egypt, 
North and South Yemen, Sudan, Somalia and Jordan, while Libya attracts work- 
ers from Egypt and Tunisia. 

Apart from the emigration of Arab labor from those countries to the Arab oil- 
producing states, there is also a similar emigration from the Arab Maghreb 
states, that 1s Morocco, Algeria and Tunisia, usually to European nations, 
especially France and West Germany. Approximately 1.5 million workers make 
up the work force from the Arab Maghreb countries. Algeria represents the 
oldest of the labor emigrating countries, as well as the largest. Its emi- 
grants go to primarily France, where it is estimated that Algerians in 1973 
made up 21 percent of the total number of foreigners in France. As for 
‘Moroccans, some sources point out that their number in Europe, by the end of 
1978, had reached 300,000, of which three-fourths were concentrated in France 
alone, while the Tunisian community in Europe numbered about 100,000 in the 
middle 70's. 

The size of the Arab labor force registered in Europe in 1978 was about 
718,000. Moreover, the estimate of Arab emigrants, working and non-working 
in 1980, was 1.7 million individuals. About 86 percent of the workers were 

concentrated in France alone, divided into 51 percent Algerians, 36.5 percent 
Moroccans and 12.5 percent Tunisians. We also find that about 86 percent of 
them work in industry and mines, while 6 percent are employed in the agri- 
cultural sector and ocean fishing, and 8 percent in services. 

Undoubtedly, a solution to this labor problem lies in directing them to avail- 
able employment opportunities in a number of Arab oil-producing countries, in- 
Stead of employing the increasing numbers of foreign laborers in these countrie 
countries, which has begun to form an increasing danger to their social struc- 
ture, apart from its other political and economic dangers. The size of the 
foreign work force in 1980 was about 2.72 million workers, of whom 950,000 

were from Asian countries. What has aided the flood of foreign workers to the 
Arab nations was allowing employers the right to obtain collective labor entry 
visas. That led to a concentration on groups, which were brought in through 
labor importers at low wages and without demands for special commitments by 

the workers. 

Eight years ago, the Arab Labor conference, in its fourth session held in 
Tripoli in 1975, decided to reconsider the aforementioned agreement and draft 
a new agreement in its place. However, the new agreement was a step backward 
from the provisions contained in the previous agreement concerning achievement 
of comprehensive unity, considering the Arab Nation an integrated economic 
unit and guaranteeing freedom of movement for labor within the Arab Nation. 
However, along with that, it contained some benefits pertaining to families 
accompanying workers and individuals of their families, as well as giving 

them the opportunity to work and study. Moreover, the agreement called on the 
signatory states to establish a tripartite (governments, workers and employers) 
organization to assume the tasks and services that signing the agreement en- 
tailed. It also gave the Arab Labor Office the job of gathering the necessary 
data to improve implementation of the agreement and coordination among the na- 
tional organizations concerned. 

Despite the passage of several years since this agreement was drafted, only a 
limited number of Arab countries have ratified it, since a majority of the 
labor-exporting countries have not ratified the amended agreement, or rather, 
some of those that ratified the first agreement have not ratified the amended 
agreement.

Saudi Arabia commercially in order to facilitate communications among other 
states, thereby realizing a financial return for both countries.

| Text | TEHRAN, Dec. 4 (Kayhan Int'l) --The Middle East and Africa are two 
Strategic axes in the foreign policy of the Islamic Republic of Iran. To this 
end the Lranian officials have always put particular emphasis on further ac- 
quaintance with the nations in these region, their governments and the poli- 
cles they adhere to. 

Hussein Sheikholeslam, the Political Undersecretary of the Lranian Foreign 
Ministry recently conducted a month long tour of a number of the Middle 
Eastern and African countries. Heading a delegation, he visited Syria, Libya, 
Algeria, Nigeria, Gabon, Madagascar, Mauritius, the People's Democratic Repub- 
lic of Yemen (DPRY), the United Arab Emirates (UAE) and Qatar. 

[In an exclusive interview with Kayhan International Sheikholeslam termed the 
results of these visits as “very useful". He also reiterated the Islamic Re- 
public's great concern for the security of the Persian Gulf and the Strait of 
Hormuz. He also warned that this Security was possible only when "our econon- 
ic interests in the region are not harmed." 

Emphasized Sheikholeslam: "If the United States or any power dare to break 
into the region, our faithful and brave people are fully prepared to fight and 
resist them, and we will do with them even worse than we did with Saddam."
