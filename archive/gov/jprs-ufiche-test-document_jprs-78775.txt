Iraq is of decisive importance in all oil conferences. Its production amounts usually 
to 2-3 million barrels daily. But the war has destroyed many of the oil installations 
and the production has dropped to a level estimated by the world oil companies at 
600,000 barrels daily. Moreover, Iraq has oil reserves that put it in fifth place 
among the oil exporting countries. These reserves amount to 30 billion barrels and 
will take 31.2 years to deplete. This depletion period puts Iraq in sixth place 
behind Iran, Kuwait, the UAE, Savdi Arabia and Libya. Moreover, Iraq has a role of 
major significance in the Gulf policies, in the development of the Gulf's economy and 
in the Arabs' military and general relationship with the resistance front against 
Israel. AL-MAJALLAH met Tayih 'Abd-al-Karim, the Iraqi minister of oil, and had the 
following interview with him: 


/Question/ Mr Minister, how would you describe Iraq's position toward the OPEC meetings? 
/Answer/ Our position is that we want OPEC's unity and solidarity and want it to 

deal with the issue of the surplus primarily and to establish a balance between the 

two ends of the equation in the market. 

/Question/ Are you for reducing the production? 

/Answer/ It is necessary. 

/Question/ What is the percentage which you view as necessary? 

/Answer/ The percentage compatible with the actual consumption. 

/Question7 Ten percent, for example? 

/Answer/ This is determined by statistics. 

/Question/7 What about the prices? 

/Answer/ The prices are subject to a number of factors, including the inflation factor.

Much of this trade is in key areas such as agriculture and communication, where 
Israeli products and expertise are rated highly. This expertise is much in demand

A project of no small economic importance was the construction in 1980 in Togo of 

a multi-million dollar factory for the production of cement clinker. Solel Boneh, 
together with the government of Ivory Coast, built this first major regional project 
in West Africa that is jointly owned by Togo, the Ivory Coast and Ghana. This 

was the first Israeli venture in Togo since ties were severed in 1973. 


Another sign of improved relations with Africa is the growing stream of young 
Africans coming to Israel to complete their studies. Although the number of 
students falls far short of the total who flocked to Israeli institutions during 
the Halcyon days of the 1960s, there are indications that more may be on the way. 
These students, whose travel and stay in Israel are often financed by international 
organizations, are enrolled in a wide variety of courses including agriculture, 
port management, adult education and trade unionism. Ironically, they make their 
way to Israel via Cairo, where they complete the last leg of the journey on a 
Nefertiti or El Al flight. 


Apart from the oil issue, there is growing West African concern of Libyan political 
ambitions in the region. Recently, the government in Accra severed diplomatic 
relations with Tripoli, accusing Colonel Mua'mmar al-Qadhdhafi's government of 
meddling in Ghana's internal affairs. 


At the diplomatic level, the Egyptian-Israeli peace treaty weakens the Arab rationale 
for withholding recognition from Israel. Africans, when discussing the matter, 

often point out that it was the Egyptians who had pressed them the hardest to break 
with Jerusalem in 1973. With an Egyptian ambassador residing in Tel Aviv, and 
Israeli occupation of the Sinai coming to an end, many black Africans no longer 
believe it is logical to hold back on formal recognition of the Jewish state. 

They recognize, of course, that the threat of sanctions, or worse, is what keeps 
their governments in line. 


Israel, for its part, is not simply waiting for the Africans to come knocking on 

the door. There was a time when Israelis, after the shock of the break had worn 

off, reacted by arguing that Israel should write off Africa. These attitudes have 
changed. Israelis have a better and more realistic appreciation of the constraints-- 
political and economic--under which African governments operate. 


Jerusalem has been using quiet diplomacy, along with trade, in an effort to nudge 
the African states towards closer relations. This policy, it can be noted, has 
begun to yield diplomatic and economic results.
