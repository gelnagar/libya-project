After the statement which al-Azhar issued on Wednesday, 9 May 1979, and 

in which it expressed the formal legal opinion that the Egyptian-Israeli 
peace treaty is correct and that the patriotivm of its signatory, namely 
President al-Sadat, is sound--after this statement, some questions were 
raised regarding the Coptic Church's position, wondering whether this 
church holds the same traditional position and asking: If the church holds 
this position, then why doesn’t it register it? 


A week after al-Azhar issued its statement, AL~KARAZAH emerged with articles 
supporting President al-Sadat's steps. 


The situation can be made clearer with the following observations: 


First observation: Like al-Azhar, which has never registered in its history 
a position against the political leadership, the Coptic Church has never 
registered such a position either. But failure to take a stance of 
opposition does not necessarily mean that these two religious establishments 





support all of the political authority's steps. This is how the situation 
was under the reign of ‘Abd-al-Nasir, whose relations with the departed 
Pope Kirillus were ideal. 


But matters became somewhat tense after the death of Pope Kirillus and Pope 
Shinudah's assumption of the papacy. 


Second observation: The source of the strain was the religious inclination 
of President al-Sadat's regime which seemed ready to apply the Islamic 
Shari'a when al-Sadat's relations with al-Qadhdhafi were flourishing. 
When the Egyptian-Libyan relations deteriorated, the tension lessened some- 
what, but this tension re-surfaced when the relations between the Kingdom 
of Saudi Arabia and Egypt flourished and when the religious inclination of 
the Egyptian regime resumed its course with the encouragement of Saudi 
Arabia. 


Throughout 3 years, there was a silent tug of war between the regime and 
the church. President al-Sadat exerted extraordinary effort to keep this 
tug of war from getting out of hand. At the outset, al-Sadat entrusted 
the task of teaching an understanding with Pope Shinudah to Prime Minister 
Mamduh Salim, and then took charge of the task personally when Mamduh 
Salim's efforts faltered at times. 


Third observation: Matters almost reached the explosion point when Pope 
Shinudah announced his fast in protest of the application of the Islamic 
laws. In this period, restlessness emerged among the Copts, who began to 
send the sect members abroad (the biggest Coptic communities are in the 
United States, Canada and Britain) tape recordings of Pope Shinudah's 
sermons in the cathedral located on Ramsis Street. This was a phenomenon 
of religious mobilization which President al-Sadat tackled personally 
with Pope Shinudah when he became aware of its sensitive nature and 
realized how it encouraged the religious current among the Moslems on the 
other hand.

"The Policemen" of the West 


Despite Sadat's vexing situation in the Arab world, he has become the 
“darling” of the media and public in the West. His prestige in the United 
States has attained heavenly heights. Carter considers himself grateful 
for Egypt's contribution to the restoration of U.S. influence in African 
countries and to the improvement of its position among the Arab states. 


Authorities in the United States still believe that only through Egypt will 
their influence be restored in the Mideast, which constitutes a vital region 
for the very existence of the United States as a world power. 


Sadat knows well what is required of him, and it is not in vain that he has 
proposed that Egypt be "the policeman" of the Western powers in Africa and 
in the Middle East. 


In return the United States is putting forth an effort to support Egypt in 
the economic area and to supply it with arms to replace increasingly 
deteriorating Soviet armaments, and all of this so that Egypt will be 
“properly capable" of fulfilling its missions that are dictated by the 
United States.

Financial Affairs 


The rulings of financial statutes in effect in the kingdom shall be applied to 
employees of the diplomatic corps in matters not provided for by this statute, 


The employees of the diplomatic corps will be paid monthly allowances according 
to their place of residence, according to the following order. 


For ambassadors to the first group of countries the allowance is 575 dinars; 

for ambassadors to the second group of countries the allowance is 550 dinars; 
for ambassadors to the third group of countries the allowance is 475 dinars; 

and for ambassadors to the fourth group of countries it is 450 dinars. For 
charges d'affaires and general consuls the allowances are 475, 375, 350, and 

225 dinars respectively; for ministers plenipotentiary and counselors it is 

450, 350, 325, 300 dinars respectively; for first and second secretaries it is 
425, 325, 300 and 275 dinars respectively; and for third secretaries ani attaches 
it is 400, 300, 275, and 250 dinars respectively. 





The first group consists of Japan. The countries of the eecond group include

gium, Lebanon, Oman, Yemen, Iran, Australia and China. The countries of the 
third group incluie Libya, Algeria, Kuwait, Bahrain, the United Arab Emirates,

tries of the fourth group inclule Egypt, Syria, the Soviet Union, Turkey, Iraq,
