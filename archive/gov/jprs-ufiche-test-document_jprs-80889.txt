The important point is that, after 1 year, ‘Ali Nasir Muhammad, president 

of the republic and chairman of the party, has done nothing except fire 
Defense Minister ‘Ali Antar for fear that he would form an alliance with his 
old military comrades against the president. The justification that was 
voiced at the time was that Antar had rebelled against the president's wish 
to execute Salih Muti', the former foreign minister who attempted to improve 
relations between Aden and Saudi Arabia. 


In any case, the dismissal of ‘Ali Antar, the most popular officer in the 
army, was considered by the Adenese to be a huge mistake on ‘Ali Nasir 
Muhammad's part, for which he might pay dearly some day. 


Officially, it is apparent that the Soviets are staying out of the Aden 
domestic dispute, but their influence over the party and the army is 

clear, especially since Moscow, according to observers, is determined to 
maintain its military and political power in Aden for strategic reasons. 


Aden's importance to the Soviets is symbolized by their retention of a 

base for their new command center on top of Jebel Hal, overlooking Aden. 
This base is protected by SAM antiaircraft missiles. This importance is 
also highlighted by Khormakasr airfield and the port. Observers say that 
Moscow was behind Aden's agreement with Libya and Ethiopia last August, 
because Aden is the point of departure for possible Soviet political infil- 
tration into Saudi Arabia, the UAE and, of course, North Yemen. Some polit- 
ical observers feel that ‘Ali Nasir Muhammad's policy with regard to wmnity 
with North Yemen has added a new factor to the internal struggle. After the 
three meetings last year that the president of South Yemen held with ‘Ali 
‘Abdallah Salih, the president of North Yemen, the two governments agreed to 
a joint interim constitution, which was issued last January, in the inter- 
ests of unity between the two Yemens. It stipulates that Sana' is to be the 
capital and Islam the state religion. It was noted that the interim con- 
stitution avoids using the word "socialism" and also avoids any mention of 
the economic program that must be followed in the unified state. 


Members of the two governments and legislators are currently discussing ways 
of reaching agreement on the final text of the joint constitution, which 
will be brought to a referendum in the two Yemens. 


Question: How do observers see the future of North Yemen? 
They would reply that the Marxist government of Aden has moved one step 


closer to control over the unified Yemeni state, while North Yemen is 
playing a very risky game. "Tt is gambling with its life." 














Some observers say that President ‘Ali ‘Abdallah Salih believes that he is 
following a suitable policy in his alliance with South Yemen and in main- 
taining his rapprochement with Saudi Arabia, which strongly subsidizes the 
North Yemen economy, as if it were its responsibility. However, observers 
have reservations about this approach. They say that ‘Ali ‘Abdallah Salih 
believes that, by using this policy, he Is trying to put pressure on Western 
nations that are hesitant about supporting him. However, his alliance with 
Aden gains more for Soviet policy than it achieves for the unity of the two 
Yemens. 


The observers add that Moscow is exploiting its friendship with South Yemen 

to penetrate into North Yemen which, despite its debts to the Soviet Union 

for the military assistance that Moscow has provided, continues to maintain 
its independence and whose foreign policy continues to be closer to the West 
and influenced by it. In addition, North Yemen has not obtained any 
guarantees from Aden on the protection of its security under the agreement, 
initiated and supported by Moscow, especially since Aden has joined with Libya 
and Ethiopia in arming the "National Democratic Front," which is aimed--as 
they say--at overthrowing the Sana’ regime and bringing a Soviet-oriented, 
Marxist government to North Yemen. 


The words of "friendship" exchanged by the two presidents last December did 
not mention either directly or indirectly Aden's support for the Northern 
rebels, who are about 8,000 strong. They control vast areas in southern 
North Yemen. Observers comment that, after this alliance, Aden might have 
asked the revolutionaries to reduce their activities, so as to give its 
alliance with Sana’ an air of good will. However, the rebels still cling to 
their positions inside North Yemen. From another angle, the friendly rela- 
tions between Sana' and Moscow, carried by ‘Ali ‘Abdallah Salih on his visit 
to the Kremlin leaders at the end of last October, have not kept Moscow from 
continuing its support for the National Democratic Front.

Moscow has 1,500 troops in South Yemen, working side by side with 4,000 

Cuban troops and 1,000 military advisers from East Germany. Moscow has

Moreover, in addition to the voices of Western observers, African alarm 
bells are beginning to go off, out of fear of Soviet expansion. Informed 
African sources have told AL-HAWADITH that the Soviet Union is in fact try- 
ing to make the southern Red Sea into a 100-percent Soviet area. In addition 
to the unlimited support for South Yemen, Moscow has built a military base on 
the Ethiopian island of Dahlak and is helping Ethiopia to occupy Eritrea, so 
that the entire Eritrean coast along the Red Sea will be under Soviet con- 
trol. Thus, Moscow can keep the states that are not friendly to it, such as 
North Yemen, Saudi Arabia, Egypt and Sudan, busy with diversionary operations-—- 
to keep them from building their societies--aimed at putting them under 
Soviet influence, and thereby achieve control over the entire Red Sea, 

being in a position to influence the Mediterranean as well as the Gulf oil 
wells. Moscow would also be able to influence some of the countries that 

use the Red Sea, such as India, Pakistan, Iran and the countries of East 
Asia, all of whom do business with and are linked to Europe. 


The African sources go on to say that "if the Red Sea falls under Soviet 
domination, that would compiete the Soviet circle in Africa, composed of 
Libya, Algeria, Benin, Chad, Angola, Mozambique, Tanzania and Uganda.”

Although the Arabic language is no longer one of the decisive criteria of labor 
preference, especially in unskilled occupations, in these Arab countries; although 
sign language takes the place of words on some occasions; although the Arabic language 
is sometimes distorted in address to facilitate understanding among Asiatics; and al- 
though some Arab countries are familiar with some Asian languages and dialects: 


In spite of all that, which might be a threat to the future of Egyptian and Arab 
workers in some of these countries, and indeed in some cases might be a threat to

there are certain occupations in which these Arab countries cannot do without people 
who speak the Arabic language articulately and fluently, especially the occupation 
of teaching at various educational levels; this is an occupation in which we are 
asking that the principle of the use of shifts be followed.
