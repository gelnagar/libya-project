Until now, the great powers and the states of the region have refrained from 
any active intervention in the conflict. For instance, at the last meeting 
of Messrs Shultz and Gromyko in October--on the occasion of the U.N. General 
Assembly meeting--the two ministers agreed that it was "necesary to put an 
end to the Iraq-Iran conflict," a position which has not varied much since 
the start of the hostilities. That position is also thet of all western 
capitals where officials state that a suspension of hostilities must be ar- 
rived at but do not take sides for Baghdad or for Tehran. Certainly, some 
Islamic countries have at times provided economic and diplomatic support to 
one or the other camp, but this support remained extremely moderate and un- 
certain, and did not have much impact on the course of fighting. Kuweit is 
said to have already lent about 4 million dollars to Baghdad—other Gulf 
countries, especially Saudi Arabia, are said to have advanced large amounts-- 
and Jordan and Egypt provided respectively, a few hundred volunteers and mili- 
tary equipment. For his part, the ayatollah's regime received expressions of 


sympathy from the Steadfastness and Confrontation Front-—Algeria, Libya, 
South Yemen, PLO and Syria--but the latter is the only one which still pub- 
licly supports Tehran in its fight against the Baghdad regime, and its 
support remains purely verbal. However, according to Mr Ariel Sharon, 
Israel's minister of defense, the Jewish state supplies limited quantities 
of arms to Iran. According to Mr Sharon, Israeli aid--which is estimated 
at 27 million dollars and includes, among other things, spare parts for the 
U.S.-made Iranian F-4 aircraft-—-has been only "symbolic" and "limited."> 
Other sources believe that it was in fact much larger. However that may be, 
the international community could soon abandon its relative neutrality, 
should the Iranian military offensive seriously threaten President Saddam 
Hussein's regime.
