The Late Muhammad Mustafa Ramadan 
Friday, 11 April was a gloomy day. It was the day the Libyan Muslim, 


Muhammad Mustafa Ramadan, was assassinated in the courtyard of the mosque 
in London's Regent Park after the Muslims had concluded their prayers. 


The murderers confessed immediately that they were al-Qadhdhafi's soldiers 
who had come to London specifically to kill a few Libyans. They acknow- 
ledged that the victim was one of those they had come to kill. 


























The series of physical eliminations inside the country and the assassi- 
nations that occurred abroad during the month of April will make the month 
of April known in the history of our country as execution month, ever since 
al-Qadhdhafi seized power by force in Libya. April will be known as the 
month of physical eliminations and nefarious assassinations. 


Muhammad Mustafa Ramadan was a Muslim in the full sense of the word. He 
was a Muslim in his purity, in his loyalty and in his moral character. He 
was a Muslim in word and deed. His Islam was marked by the spirit of the 
age and by a sense of cultural openness. 


Muhammad Mustafa Ramadan came from a well-known family in Tripoli, the 

ibn Ramadan family. His father went to Egypt when the country was ruled 

by Italians and returned to Libya in 1953. Muhammad was eight years old 
then. His father Lived with his sister in *”2 old city, and Muhammad 
attended Libyan schools and Tripoli Secondary School. He appeared to be 
very fond of Islamic studies, and he began to write during the third prepa- 
ratory grade. He took an active part in writing newsletters, and he became 
a prominent announcer on Libyan radio. He left school to work full time 

as an announcer on Libyan radio. In 1965 he was summoneu by Sadiq 
Khashkhushah, who was then director of investigations, and he was ques- 
tioned about his activities. His services then became unsatisfactory, so he 
joined the British Broadcasting Corporation, the BBC, in London where he 
worked tor 4 years. In 1970 his contract [with the BBC] expired, but he 
continued to work from abroad for the BBC as an announcer and a program 
host. 


In 1971 he joined the University of Rabat in Morocco as a part-time 
student. He would travel to Rabat to take the examination, and [he con- 
tinued to do that] until he received his degree in law. 


Muhammad Mustafa Ramadan became interested in the 1969 coup in L.dya, and 
the change gave him an incentive to visit the country. In 1970 he visited 
Libya for 1 week and returned to announce that military rule had begun 

in Libya and that a gruesome era was awaiting our people. He did not stop 
voicing this opinion. 


Muhammad Mustafa Ramadan was not a fanatic man. He was rather confident 

of his ability to take part in a discussion or a dialogue and, therefore, 
he used to follow the news of the country on a daily basis and to meet and 
become acquainted with everyone. He even made the acquaintance of 
al-Qadhdhafi's cousin, Sayyid Qadhdhaf al-Damm when [the latter] was mili- 
tary attache in London. It was said then that Qadhdhaf al-Damm had 
delivered messages to al-Qadhdhafi and that tempting offers had arrived 
from al-Qadhdhafi to Ramadan. Among those offers were one to let Ramadan 
take over Libyan radio and another to let him take over the radio broadcast 
in Malta. However, Muhammad was careful not to let himself slide into 
serving the regime, for his mission [as he saw it] was to make the acquain- 
tance of the regime's key figures and inner circle without becoming 
involved with them in any cooperative effort that would serve the interests 
of the regime. 




















Muhammad lived in poverty and died in poverty. He was extremely devoted 
to the noble concepts of his Islamic faith, to his peace of mind and to the 
sacrifices he made to promote truth. In those respects he was a wealthy man. 


He used to boast of his opposition to military rule anywhere in our Islamic 
world, and he . so used to boast of his hostility to those who exploited 
religion to susiain their government. He was a revolutionary Muslim, but 
the time he spent on branding the Libyan regime was only marginal. Islam 
was his cause, and his opinion of the Libyan regime or his criticism of it 
was made in the context of discussions about military Fascists who are 
enemies of freedom and of Islam. 


He was always eager tu know who was the opposition that was publishing 
AL-JIHAD. He was hoping to make their acquaintance so he can engage them in 
a discussion and perhaps contribute to the newspaper, but circumstances did 
not make that possible. Muhammad was a true patriot and a constant champion 
of truth and goodness. 


Muhammad had a sense of humor. He would occasionally tell jokes, and he 
liked to use the citle, "Afandi,"' in addressing his dear friends. He liked 
to read, and he wrote continuously. He wrote to newspapers from Kuwait to 
Morocco to comment on their articles. He spent most of his limited tunds to 
buy paper, newspapers and magazines. 


He published three books, [which included] articles and replies to a few 
Arab writers. He opposed military rule everywhere. 


Our pledge to the late Ramadan and to those who lost their lives before 

him and to those who will lose their lives after him is that we will con- 
tinue to fight with all the methods and means [at our disposal] against the 
brutal, terrorist regime of al-Qadhdhafi. We will not rest until he is

April, a Month of Bloodshed and Martyrs 


In April 1977 the Fascist al-Qadhdhafi began to glower more ciearly. He 
executed Libyan citizens--students, officers and simple citizens; some were 
shot and some were hanged. 


In April 1980 another wave of bloodshed began inside the country and abroad. 


It began under the slogan or the cover of "fighting bribery among the 
pillars of the regime." The Fascist then went about eliminating officers, 
incellectuals and former party members who had left political activity 

and gone to build for themselves a quiet life away from any [political | 
posture. But al-Qadhdhafi was promoting the slogan of he who is not with us 
is against us. That is, he who did not spend his time applauding him and 
cheering for him was his enemy. 


In the decrees he issued in February 1980 al-Qadhdhafi announced under 
the cover of the so-called revolutionary committees the onset of a stage of 
eliminating Libyans abroad. 


Thus once again it was not enough to be abroad and not to be working 
against him, but the fact of one’s mere presence abroad was [construed as] 
disobedience to him and an expression of one's hostility to his regime. The 
mere fact that a Libyan had settled abroad was considered by al-Qadhdhafi 
to be a declaration of hostility by that person. Therefore, all such people 
were to be eliminated, and the first to go would be those who were more 
accessible, the more famous ones and those who were better known to people. 


Thus the suicide squad began traveling 1 week before the resolutions of the 
revolutionary committees were announced. It arrived in London, Paris, 
Geneva, Athens, Beirut, Bonn, Madrid and in the United States. 


If Libyans living abroad were to be eliminated regardless of their attitude 
toward the regime, there was no longer any alternative but to sacrifice 

time and money to oppose the existing regime as long as that regime did not 
hesitate to murder anyone who was living abroad regardless of his position. 


Salim al-Ratimi, a merchant who was not involved in politics, was assassina- 
ted; ‘Abd-al-Jalil ‘Arf, a merchant who was not politically involved, 

was assassinated; Muhammad Mustafa Ramadan, who gave his time to the cause 
of [slam in exile and who had left Libya many years before al-Qadhdhafi 

had come to power, was assassinated. 


Other people have been assassinated [also], and other assassination 
attempts are in the offing. 


Inside the country al-Qadhdhafi eliminated those who refused to cooperate 
with the regime even though they were devoted to their families, their 
businesses and their trade. Others were taken to detention camps—-there 
are 4,000 detainees--for no reason other than that they did not applaud 
the regime and cheer for it in processions.

Human Rights and Basic Freedoms in al-Qadhdhafi's Dictatorship 


This report offers a quick survey and a brief review of human rights viola- 
tions in al-Qadhdhafi's al-Jamahiriyah. Al-Qadhdhafi has been ruling Libya 
since 1 September 1969. It was then that the people woke up to [the sounds 
of ] military music and the proclamation of a republic which had followed an 
announcement that the royal regime had been overthrown. The first and last 
king of that royal regime was King Muhammad Idris ai-Mahdi al-Sanusi who 
was crowned king of the country when Libya's independence was proclaimed on 
24 December 1952. 


Regardless of the political aspects and secrets that accompanied this event 
or led to it, al-Qadhdhafi, who was appointed chairman of the Revolutionary 
Command Council and promoted from first lieutenant to colonel on the eighth 
day of the revolution, is the one who continued to rule the country until 
this day. His autocratic control of the government is increasing daily. 
This [situation] has led to a lengthy and horrible series of acts of 
violence, oppression, repression, imprisonment and execution that are being 
carried out against citizens. This includes al-Qadhdhafi's colleagues who 
took part in the coup with him as well as civilians who were led to jail 
under different circumstances and detained for many years. They were all 
tried by special military courts, and tyrannical measures were taken 
against them in violation of the simplest legal principles and guarantees. 


We are not concerned at present with a political opinion of al-Qadhdhafi's 
regime as much as we are trying to conduct a detached and fair inves- 
tigation of the position of al-Qadhdhafi's regime on the universal decla- 
ration of human rights and of basic and political freedoms for citizens. We 
are supporting our report with documents issued by al-Qadhdhafi's regime. 
These documents consist of the following: 


First, the statements al-Qadhdhafi made in numerous speeches and in his 
statements to newspapers and broadcasts. 


Second, al-Qadhdhafi's laws and decrees which indicate a blatant violation 
of the Libyan citizen's basic human rights. 


Third, the practices of al-Qadhdhafi and his regime against the citizens 
when his statements, his speeches, his laws and his decrees are put into 
practice. 


Since this report will be backed up by the three aforementioned references, 
it has thus avoided as much as possible [basing its statements on] poli- 
tical opinion. The report adheres to objectivity and respects the spirit of 
scientific inquiry with regard to human rights. In this regard it is ser- 
ving the interests of historical truths. 


In the past, during 18 years of the royal regime the Libyan people have 
not had to face a wave of violence, repression, emergency courts and execu- 
tions such as that which they are facing at present. Regardless of the 
government's political form there was one constitution which provided 




















gu-*antees for citizens, such as the right to trial in a common court. No 
one was taken to court, and throughout that administration, not a single 
civilian or military emergency court was convened. 


No one was arrested by any means other than those of the common publi-= 
prosecution. At that time there weren't many judicial agencies: common and 
emergency; political and non-political. 


No one has ever established that the royal regime tortured any citizen, 
unless we were to consider in that regard a brawl between an officer and 
a prisoner due to some friction between them or to personal attack. 


No one's home was broken into to be searched unless a warrant from the 
public prosecutor's office had been obtained and all the legal measures in 
etfect had been taken. 


No one was ever accused or condemned except in accordance with the uniform 
penal code whose provisions comprise all violations and whose purpose is 
not particulary to protect the regime or fight its opponents. 


None of this means that there were no excesses. Nor does this mean that 
the former administration was perfect or ideal.

Although parties were not allowed to exist and, consequently, there were no 
organized opposition parties, the administration did not allow and did not 
try to form its own party that would have sole control over the government 
or force itself upon the people. There was, however, a parliament to which

legislative powers: it could enact laws after debating them and voting over 
them. The parliament also had the right to withdraw confidence from the 
prime minister and from the Council of Ministers. 


The judicial authority was independent in accordance with the constitution. 
lt managed its own affairs through its own higher council, the Supreme 
Court Council. There was also a supreme court which considered suits filed 
by persons against any violation of the constitution. The supreme court 

had the right to rule that laws, statutes and administrative orders were 
unconstitutional if they violated the constitution or the rights guaranteed 
therein. 


The political analysis of the reasons for that administration's severe 
shortcomings in not allowing the creation of parties and in [tolerating] 
other excesses may go on at some length, but we are not engaged in a pro- 
cess of presenting a political critique of this administration or of 
al-Qadhdhafi's regime as much as we are concerned with an analysis of the 
constitutional and legal reality and the violations of the principles of 
human rights that are being committed by al-Qadhdhafi's regime, his con- 
stitution, his laws and his practices. 


It is also not the purpose of this article to make a comparison between 

two regimes. This article rather introduces us to the life which the Libyan 
citizen lived by [describing to us] the constitutional and legal form and 
substance [of that time] so we can compare them with those of the present. 
Briefly, there was a constitution which provided basic rights for citizens 
and [stipulated] the form of the state and its regime which was based on 
the independence of the legislative authority as vested in a bicameral 
parliament; of the executive authority, vested in the Council of ministers 
that would answer to the house of representatives which had the right to 
debate, endorse and decree laws, the state's budget and foreign and 
domestic policy; and of the judicial authority which monitored the applica-
