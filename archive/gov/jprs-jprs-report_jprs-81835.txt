IRAN 


REPORT ON KHARG ISLAND JETTY 'HIT BY IRAQI MISSILES' 
London IRAN PRESS SERVICE in English 26 Aug 82 p 3 


[Text] Reports from Iran indicated this week that Iraqi missiles had hit 

the main jetty at Iran's main oil-loading port on Kharg Island in the Persian 
Gulf. It could be two weeks before it can be working fully again, sources 
added. 


But the other two main loading jetties, the Dariush and the Sea Island are 
still operating, the reports continued. But the fact that the Iraqis could 
hit the island at such range is causing new concern to Iranian oil officials. 


Tehran sources claim that the missiles that have struck the island were 
supplied by the French, and the Iraqis were using them to bring new pressure 
on Tehran to make peace. 


Iran's efforts to barter oil for vital imports seems to have hit snags. Reports 
claim that New Zealand meat suppliers have already told the Iranians that they 
will have to pay cash rather than oil to obtain supplies. 


Similarly, some Italian suppliers have informed Iranians that the barter 
arrangements could no longer work and that they preferred to be paid in cash. 


Last week Oil Minister Mohammad Gharazi was in Libya, apparently to discuss the 
next summit of the non-aligned countries, but sources in Tehran said he was 
asking for loans from Kaddafi. It is believed that because Iran can no longer 
barter oil for goods it needs it has been trying to raise funds in some friendly 
countries. (Baghdad late Wednesday claimed its planes had damaged Kharg 
facilities further.)

ATTACKS ON REVOLUTIONARY GUARDS INTENSIFY 
London IRAN PRESS SERVICE in English 26 Aug 82 p 3, 4 


[Text] According to reports from Tehran, as many as 5,000 newly-recruited 
revolutionary guards have been brought from the provinces to Tehrar to fill 
gaps created by desertions and guards killed by the regime's opponents. 


One report said that the authorities were so worried about losing their grip 
on Tehran that they were prepared to risk weakening their position in the 
provinces. The guards in the capital usually travel in jeeps in large groups 
and do not effectively patrol streets. 


During the past few weeks at least 35 guards have been killed by assailants. 
In several instances these guards were captured while they were off duty and 
in civilian clothes. The usual practice was to kidnap the guards and then 
kill them with knives and axes, and then send parts of their bodies to the 
houses of other guards. 


Bodies of young men with the marks of torture on them, exhumed from graveyards, 
have also been found left in streets of Tehran, reports from the capital say.

WRITER DISCUSSES CURRENT CHALLENGES, EFFECT OF WAR 
Paris EUROPE OUTREMER in French No 626-627, Mar-Apr 82 pp 11-13 
[Article by R. T.: "Morocco Confronted With Four Challenges" 


[Text] In the course of its history, every country is 
confronted with political, economic or social problems. 
Some are due to internal causes, others to external 
causes; some have to do with the current economic situa- 
tion, others are of a more permanent nature. Today, every 
country is going through the international economic 
recession but the most seriously affected are those Third 
World countries which do not produce oil. 


Morocco falis in the latter category, even if the World Bank groups it in the 
middle-income category, between the poorest and the richest countries, with a 
national per capita product of $860 in 1980. In Africa, among the 50 OAU 
member countries last 1 January, it appears, still according to this criterion, 
in 10th place, after the oil-producing countries (Nigeria, Libya, Algeria, 
Tunisia and Gabon) and after the Seychelles, Ivory Coast, Mauritius and 
Botswana. Hence, despite the size of its population, it is in the top 

group. However, today Morocco has to face four major challenges, two 
endogenous ones: the population explosion and food self-sufficiency; and two 
exogenous ones: the world crisis and the Sahara war. 


The Population Explosion 


The tirst challenge is by far the most important because it is of a permanent 
nature and weighs more and more heavily on development and the standard of 
living. All the earnings from production are consumed by growing needs. 

One need only move about the cities and countryside to note the swarm of 
children and the youthfulness of the inhabitants. 


The third general population census since independence wili take place next 
September: it is eagerly awaited because it is expected to provide a large 
amount of socioeconomic data vital for planning. In the meantime, only 
estimates are available. The 1980 population was estimated at 20.1 million 
people, of whom 58 percent were under 20 years old, those over 64 representing 
only 2.7 percent of the total. The annual rate of growth is officially 2.9
