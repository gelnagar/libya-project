An Open Question 


It is an interesting paradox that it was precisely under these circumstances that 
one of the most important breakthroughs in the history of the Zionist enterprise 
was accomplished: The gates of Russia were opened for significant immigration of 
Jews to Israel. (We will not deal here with the distressing paradox that most of 
these Jews today are not joining us.) 


It is difficult to determine if Soviet accomplishments in the Arab world since 
1967 were precisely the result of its breaking relations with Israel or would have 
been smaller but for that break. It could also be claimed that in so doing it 
lost a vital lever in its political game with the Arab countries. Its success 
thus far has not been as great as one might have supposed. The bottom line is 
that the principal large Arab states have remained outside its sphere of influence 
and its active partnership is mainly with crumbling or crisis-ridden regimes like 
Syria, Libya and South Yemen. Beyond that, it also had no part in the peace 
process that began with President Sadat's visit to Jerusalem. The price it pays 
as a result of a lack of direct channels of communication between itself and Is- 
rael includes, among other things, a reduction in its maneuverability vis-a-vis 
the Arabs and its inferior position in comparison with the United States, which 
has access to all the sides involved in the conflict in the area. The power and 
dimensions of the two countries, the USSR and Israel, are obviously totally dif- 
ferent from one another, but one of the characteristics of the situation that was 
created after the break in relations between them is the growth in the relative 
dependence of each of them on one of the active sides in the Middle East: Israel 
on the United States and the USSR on the Arab countries. It is an open question 
as to how much an improvement in relations between them would reduce this relative 
dependence.
